Picture a kind little error message. “Oops, something went wrong. Try again or contact support.” Warm, no blame, and no jargon. I’d ship it any day, minus the “oops.”

Now hand that line to an AI agent that’s paying a bill for someone. It reads the message and has no idea what to do with it. No reason. No next step. Just a friendly nudge to try again. So it tries again. And again. Ten times, until the bank’s safety limit kicks in and freezes the customer’s payment.

Now, you have a bad experience. And rest assured, everyone will be quick to blame AI. But AI did its job. So did the content designer. The problem was no one had planned how they’d work together.

Think about that line. It was kind to a person but useless to a machine. And a machine was the one driving.

That copy didn’t fail because it was badly written. Nobody had decided what should happen when a payment fails. The warm sentence was covering for a decision no one made. A person would never notice. And the machine? Well, **_it_** can’t do anything but show you.

A year ago I wrote that [AI had started reading our content](https://uxcontent.com/content-design-isnt-about-words-and-never-was/) before any human saw it. Summarizing our notifications, like the Apple summary that turned a comedian’s great night into a death announcement. Rewriting our emails into a quick preview. That felt like a big shift. It went a lot further. The agent reads the words, acts on them, and tells your customer what happened in its own voice.

Most content teams haven’t zoomed out on that yet.

## The agent gets there first

Picture someone opening a savings account. They used to search, land on your page, compare, decide who to trust, and then click. Every word we wrote was aimed at that person. We even had personas plastered across our working spaces to remind us.  
  
That world just tipped. Last week, Cloudflare reported that bots and AI agents now generate more web traffic than humans do, a crossover its own CEO didn’t expect until 2027. By volume, the majority reader of the web’s content is already a machine. Yours included.

Now they can ask an AI agent. “Hey Claude, find me a high-yield savings account. No monthly fees. Automatic transfers are a must.” The AI agent does the whole thing. Reads the pages, compares the rates, checks the fine print, picks one, opens the account. The person never sees your page at all.

The agent made the call. That changes what your content even is. It stops being marketing and becomes the thing a machine has to read correctly and act on. And a machine reads nothing like we do. A person reads between the lines. A machine only reads the lines.

## And it speaks for you, too

When an AI agent reports back to the person, it puts your product in its own words. Your content becomes its script. Write clearly and it describes you the way you’d want. Write vaguely and it fills the blank with a guess, says that guess to your customer in a calm, confident voice, and you never see it happen.

This already happened in real life. Last year, users of Cursor, an AI coding tool, started getting logged out when they switched machines. They emailed support and got a confident answer from “Sam”: the logouts were expected behavior under the new one-device login policy.

Sounds completely reasonable, right? Well, it was completely invented. There was no such policy, and no Sam. An AI support bot signing as “Sam” had filled the gap with a rule that sounded right, and customers believed it. Some canceled their subscriptions over a policy that never existed, and a co-founder ended up on Reddit doing damage control.

Nobody at Cursor wrote that policy. The bot hit a question it couldn’t answer and did what these models do. They match patterns. They don’t grasp what’s true. And when they get it wrong, they say it with the exact confidence they’d use for something real.

Which means you’re writing the lines an AI agent will say **_for_** you, to your customer, when you’re not in the room.

## This is the new SEO

Search did this to us once already. Everyone suddenly cared about keywords and structure. Strategy bent around being seen.

Now it’s happening again, bigger. This time it isn’t about ranking. It’s about whether an agent can understand what you do and trust it enough to recommend you to the next person who asks. And it’s reading everything. Your pricing page, sure, but also your product pages and your onboarding, the same copy a person skims in two seconds.

Lean too hard on brand voice, or a metaphor that only half-says the thing, and it can still charm a human while sailing right over the agent’s head. The agent can’t tell what you actually offer, so it leaves you out. Not ranked low. Gone. The customer never learns you were an option.

[Adobe found AI-driven traffic to retail sites jumped 693% over the 2025 holidays](https://business.adobe.com/au/blog/ai-driven-traffic-surges-across-industries), and it kept climbing into 2026. Their other finding is the one content designers should take note of: most sites still aren’t built for a machine to read.

Translate: the demand showed up, but the content didn’t.

AI agents compare notes, too. One tries your cancel flow, hits a wall, and that gets passed along. To the person, sure. Maybe to the next agent deciding whether you’re worth the trouble. Your clarity becomes a kind of word of mouth, except the mouths are machines and you can’t hear the conversation.

## The real shift: persuasion to instruction

For decades we wrote to persuade. Now we also write to be carried out. Persuasion to instruction. That one shift changes what “good” even means.

And it keeps landing in the same place. Every spot where a machine stumbles, you’ll find a decision nobody made. What happens when the payment fails. What to say when the answer isn’t known. For years, vague copy hid those holes. A person fills the blank on instinct and moves on, so no one ever had to decide. The machine can’t fill it. Now somebody has to, and that somebody will be staring at your words.

Which means the job was never really about phrasing. It was about pinning down what’s true and writing it so plainly any AI agent can act on it.

People want to make this “write for humans or write for machines.” It isn’t that. Clear words serve both. The warmth I used to fight for on operational copy was mostly decoration, and it was already losing the humans who skim. Now AI agents are sending us the bill.

The catch is knowing who’s on the other end. The operational stuff, the errors and statuses and fees and limits, needs to go machine-first now. Plain and exact, every number named. The brand moments a person actually reads, a welcome or a thank-you, keep their warmth. The job is telling which is which, and writing for that reader.

## What this looks like in the real world

My team has a rule about exclamation points. Two of them. Not per page. Per designer, per year. Too much enthusiasm reads as noise, so we ration it hard. That’s a rule for humans.

Lately we’ve been writing a different rulebook. We’re tracking hundreds of content rules now, built into the AI assistants the team has set up and works with, and more of them every week. We wrote them so an agent has rules to act on, not just vibes to guess from. Now those rules drive a broader mission: talking to an agent instead of a person.

The one rule I’d put on the wall: name the thing before you shorten it. Say “the refund of $47.32” the first time, and then you’ve earned an “it.” Not “it’ll be processed and it’ll show up in a few days,” where nobody, human or machine, can tell which “it” is which. A person shrugs and figures it out. An agent can’t, and that loose pronoun is how it calls the wrong tool or freezes on a step that doesn’t exist.

One thing caught me off guard, and it stung a little. The copy that felt cold to me, the version stripped of the warmth I used to spend hours getting right, was the clearest one for the agent. No feeling to wade through. The reassurance I was proud of was the exact thing the machine had to fight past.

## Start with one flow

You don’t need an “AI content strategy” to start thinking this way. You just need words a machine can actually read.

The fastest gut check I know: paste your copy into Claude and ask it to sum up the message in one line. If the summary drops the point or flips the meaning, the words aren’t ready. Front-load harder and run it again.

Then pick one flow. Something like a product checkout, or a cancel subscription. Read the whole flow out loud in the way the agent would: no instincts, no patience, just the words on the screen, and you have to take the right action.

The old question was “is this readable?” The new one is “can a machine act on this safely?” Find the spot where “submit” could mean two things. Find the error that says “something went wrong” instead of what went wrong and what to do next.

Take the line we opened with. “Oops, something went wrong. Try again.” Rewrite it for a machine. “Your payment didn’t go through. The bank declined the card. Try a different one.” Still kind. Now there’s a reason and a next step, so the agent stops looping and does the right thing. And the person knows what happened too. One rewrite, both readers.

Then go after the high-stakes content first. The policies, the fees, the limits, the rules. Stop writing them as long, friendly paragraphs. Write them so the action and the outcome are impossible to misread. A machine can act on that. So can a person.

Your final gut check? Where is the fluff? If the content has any fluff, remove it. No exceptions. That one phrase your marketing partner added to “make it pop” is now the AI agent content killer.

## Why this is the best thing that’s happened to our job

The fear I’m hearing everywhere is that AI replaces content design. I think it’s the opposite. As AI becomes the way people reach a product, the words behind it carry more weight, not less. The agent only knows what your content tells it. Vague words in, vague agent out. Clear words in, clear agent out.

And I can tell you what this looks like from inside a team that uses AI every day. The work didn’t shrink. It grew. Every flow now has a second reader to write for. Every rule we write down surfaces ten more we never had time to define. The drafts come faster, sure. But a faster draft just gets you to the real work sooner, deciding what’s true, what’s allowed, what happens when it fails. Those decisions were always the job. There are just more of them now, and they matter more.

There’s no model clever enough to rescue copy that means two things. The meaning has to live in the words, and putting it there was always our job.

I keep saying we don’t write the genie’s script. We teach it how to talk. Now we teach it how to listen too, and how to speak for us when we’re not in the room.

But the bigger thing is what content designers have known for years. The writing usually isn’t the problem. When a machine can’t act on your words, it’s usually not that a content designer didn’t “do it right.” It’s that the company never decided what it actually does. The vague sentence was the hiding place. The agent just turned the lights on.

That used to be invisible work. Now it’s the strategy work. If you lead a content team, this is the quarter to reread your operational copy with no instincts and no patience, the way an agent will.

We spent years asking people to take words seriously. The machines already do.
