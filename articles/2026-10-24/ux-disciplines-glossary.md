Summary:  Definitions of fields related to user experience, including UX design, UI design, visual design, interaction design, service design, UX research, and DesignOps

People often use terms such as _UX_, _UI_, _CX_, and _visual design_ interchangeably, even though they refer to different fields of practice. This glossary focuses primarily on what each kind of work involves. Some of these terms (such as _user experience_ and _customer experience_) also have other common meanings. For example, _user experience_ can refer to the field of user experience or to the experience of users interacting with a particular product, while _information architecture_ can refer to the field of information architecture or the content organization on a specific site. Where useful, we note these additional meanings.

Job titles in our field are not standardized. In our [UX-careers survey](https://www.nngroup.com/reports/user-experience-careers/), respondents reported [134 unique job titles related to UX](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-career-advice/). As a result, the boundaries described here are the typical ones; in any given organization, one person may do the work of several of these areas, and the same title may mean different things.

Some fields in this glossary (e.g., market research) are not traditionally part of UX but are adjacent to it and often confused with it; others (e.g., UX research) are subfields of UX.

### Agent Experience (AX)

**Agent experience (AX)** is the practice of designing digital products and services so that they can be effectively understood and operated by AI agents when acting on behalf of people. Agent experience can include structuring information so that agents can interpret it reliably, tool descriptions and permissions that enable agents to operate, and APIs that allow agents to directly access an offering, without relying on a human-facing user interface.

-   See also: [user experience (UX)](#user_experience)

### Content Design

**Content design** is the practice of determining what information users need, when they need it, and how to best provide it. It involves selecting, structuring, formatting, and presenting content based on user needs and context. Content designers usually have skills in both writing and design and may be responsible for information architecture, [wireframing](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/prototypes-vs-wireframes-ux-projects/), and [prototyping](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-prototype-hi-lo-fidelity/). Content design is broader than UX writing, which focuses on the words in the interface.

-   See also: [UX writing](#ux_writing), [content strategy](#content_strategy), [information architecture](#information_architecture)
-   Related article: [**Content Strategy vs. UX Writing**](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/content-strategy-vs-ux-writing/)

### Content Strategy

**Content strategy** is the ongoing practice of planning for the creation, delivery, and governance of useful, usable, and effective content. A content strategy specifies the purpose of the content, who owns and maintains it, its format and distribution, how it is organized, and how it fits into the overall user experience. In other words, content strategy is concerned with the processes around content, whereas UX writing and content design produce the content.

-   See also: [content design](#content_design), [UX writing](#ux_writing)
-   Related article: [**Content Strategy 101**](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/content-strategy/)

### Conversation Design

**Conversation design** is the discipline of designing the exchanges between users and systems that communicate in natural language, such as [chatbots](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/chatbots/), [voice assistants](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/voice-assistant-attitudes/), and [AI assistants built on large language models](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ai-chatbots-design-guidelines/). It covers what the system says, how it asks for and confirms information, and what happens when it misunderstands the user. Conversation design combines interaction design and UX writing for interfaces in which the conversation is the main (or only) UI.

-   See also: [interaction design](#interaction_design), [UX writing](#ux_writing), [agent experience](#agent_experience)
-   Related article: [**The User Experience of Chatbots**](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/chatbots/)

### Customer Experience (CX)

**Customer experience (CX)** has two meanings. As a discipline, CX is the practice of studying and improving the relationship between customers and an organization over the lifetime of that relationship.

As an experience, CX is the totality of a customer's interactions with an organization over time, across every touchpoint: advertising, purchase, customer support, post-purchase followup, and so on. UX and CX overlap considerably. Today, some people use “UX” to refer to the experience of a single type of interaction with a product (e.g., registering on a mobile app) and “CX” to refer to all interactions throughout the customer relationship.

-   See also: [user experience](#user_experience), [service design](#service_design), [employee experience](#employee_experience)
-   Related article: [**User Experience vs. Customer Experience: What's the Difference?**](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-vs-cx/)

### Design Operations (DesignOps)

**Design operations (DesignOps)** is the orchestration and optimization of people, processes, and craft to amplify design's value and impact at scale. In other words, DesignOps creates the team structures, workflows, and measures that allow a design team to do good work as it grows, so that designers can focus on designing. Common DesignOps roles include design program manager and design producer.

-   See also: [research operations](#research_operations), [UX design](#ux_design)
-   Related article: [**DesignOps 101**](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/design-operations-101/)

### Employee Experience (EX)

**Employee experience (EX)** has two meanings. As a discipline, EX involves studying and improving employees' experience of working for an organization, including the internal tools, processes, policies, and physical spaces they deal with.

As an experience, EX refers to employees' perceptions of their interactions with the organization. EX applies to employees the same approach that CX applies to customers. It is often owned by human resources; UX teams usually contribute through service design and through the design of internal software and intranets.

-   See also: [customer experience](#customer_experience), [service design](#service_design)

### Experience Design (XD)

**Experience design (XD)** is an approach to creating high-quality, optimized experiences that encompasses all levels of the user's experience: individual interactions with specific products, the user's journey over time, and the customer's lifetime relationship with the organization. The term is used as an umbrella for UX, CX, service design, and product design, which share a focus on people, structured problem solving, iteration, and methods such as user research and journey mapping. In practice, _experience designer_ is often an alternative job title for UX designer.

-   See also: [user experience](#user_experience), [customer experience](#customer_experience), [service design](#service_design), [product design](#product_design)
-   Related article: [**Experience Design: The Next Iteration of UX?**](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/experience-design/)

### Graphic Design

**Graphic design** is the discipline of communicating information visually through typography, imagery, color, and layout, in any medium: print, packaging, signage, advertising, and digital. Graphic design is much older than digital interfaces. Visual design in UX applies many of the principles of graphic design to interactive products.

-   See also: [visual design](#visual_design), [UI design](#user_interface_design)
-   Related article: [**5 Principles of Visual Design in UX**](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/principles-visual-design/)

### Human Factors and Ergonomics

**Human factors and ergonomics** is the discipline that studies how people interact with the systems, tools, and environments around them. It applies what it learns about human physical and cognitive capabilities and limitations to the design of equipment, systems, and environments, with the goal of improving both human and system performance. It is often practiced in safety-critical domains such as aviation, healthcare, automotive design, and industrial control. The discipline of human factors predates UX, and several UX methods (such as [task analysis](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/task-analysis/)) originated at least partly in human-factors work.

-   See also: [human-computer interaction](#human_computer_interaction), [user experience](#user_experience)

### Human-Computer Interaction (HCI)

**Human-computer interaction (HCI)** is the academic discipline that studies how people use computers and how to design computing systems that people can use. It draws on computer science, cognitive psychology, and design. HCI is primarily an academic-research field; UX is mainly a professional field. The two overlap substantially, and UX draws heavily on HCI theories and methods.

-   See also: [human factors and ergonomics](#human_factors_and_ergonomics), [user experience](#user_experience), [UX research](#ux_research)

### Information Architecture (IA)

**Information architecture (IA)** is the practice of deciding how to organize and maintain the content of a website or application, the relationships among pieces of content, and how content is displayed in the navigation UI. IA is not part of the on-screen user interface; rather, IA informs the UI.

IA also refers to how the content and functionality of a particular site or application are organized and related.

-   See also: [content design](#content_design), [interaction design](#interaction_design), [UX design](#ux_design)
-   Related article: [**Information Architecture vs. Sitemaps**](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/information-architecture-sitemaps/)

### Interaction Design (IxD)

**Interaction design (IxD)** is the discipline of designing how a product and its users respond to each other: what actions users can take, what feedback the system gives, and how the system’s state changes as a result of a user action. Interaction designers work on flows, controls, states, and behaviors. In contrast, visual design determines how these elements look.

-   See also: [UI design](#user_interface_design), [visual design](#visual_design), [conversation design](#conversation_design), [motion design](#motion_design)

### Market Research

**Market research** refers to collecting information about markets, customers, competitors, demand, and attitudes to inform business decisions such as segmentation, pricing, positioning, and market strategy. In contrast, UX research studies users — their needs, behaviors, and interactions with a product or service — to inform design decisions. The two fields use many of the same research methods.

-   See also: [UX research](#ux_research), [customer experience](#customer_experience), [marketing](#marketing)

### Marketing

**Marketing** is the discipline of positioning, communicating, and delivering products or services that provide both customer and business value. It includes understanding the market (i.e., market research), attracting potential customers, and engaging existing customers.

-   See also: [market research](#market_research), [user experience](#user_experience)
-   Related article: [**UX & Marketing: Balancing Business Goals and Supporting Users**](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-and-marketing/)

### Motion Design

**Motion design** is the discipline of designing animations and transitions. In UX, motion is used mainly to give feedback that the system recognized an action, to communicate state changes, to help users understand where they are in a hierarchy, and to signal how users can interact with an element. Outside UX, motion design also includes animated graphics for video and advertising.

-   See also: [interaction design](#interaction_design), [visual design](#visual_design)
-   Related article: [**The Role of Animation and Motion in UX**](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/animation-purpose-ux/)

### Product Design

**Product design**, in the software industry, refers to designing a digital product from end to end, with attention to business goals and product strategy, as well as to users. _Product designer_ is often a job title for work that is similar to UX design but is concentrated on a single product; in some organizations, it implies more responsibility for the UI or a closer partnership with product management. (Outside software, product design usually refers to the design of physical objects.) Product design is different from **product management**, which is responsible for deciding what product to build and why (for example, setting the product strategy, roadmap, and priorities).

-   See also: [UX design](#ux_design), [UI design](#user_interface_design), [product management](#product_management)
-   Related article: [**Product and UX: Study Guide**](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/product-and-ux-study-guide/)

### Product Management

**Product management** is the discipline responsible for creating a product strategy that delivers value to the business and to users, communicating that strategy to partners, and seeing it through to execution. Product managers decide what the product should do and why: they set the product vision and roadmap, prioritize features, and are usually accountable for the product's business results. Product management is different from product design and UX design, which determine how the product works for its users; the roles overlap in areas such as discovery and prioritization, and in most organizations product managers and designers work closely together.

-   See also: [product design](#product_design), [UX design](#ux_design), [UX research](#ux_research)
-   Related article: [**The Product-Manager Archetype**](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/product-manager-archetype/)

### Research Operations (ResearchOps or ReOps)

**Research operations (ResearchOps)** is the orchestration and optimization of people, processes, and craft to amplify the value and impact of research at scale. ResearchOps covers 6 areas: participants (recruiting, screening, scheduling, and compensating), governance (consent, privacy, and information storage), knowledge (collecting, synthesizing, and sharing insights), tools, competency (educating and onboarding others to do research), and advocacy (communicating the value of research in the organization). ResearchOps is often considered part of DesignOps.

-   See also: [design operations (DesignOps)](#design_operations), [UX research](#ux_research)
-   Related article: [**ResearchOps 101**](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/research-ops-101/)

### Service Design

**Service design** is the discipline of planning and designing how people, processes, systems, and touchpoints work together to deliver a service. It considers the customers’ experience, the employees’ experience, and the organizational processes that make that experience possible. Service designers usually document a business’s resources (people, props, and processes) and how they relate to each other in service blueprints. Whereas UX is primarily focused on what the end user encounters, service design also considers how that user experience is created behind the scenes, inside the organization.

-   See also: [customer experience](#customer_experience), [employee experience](#employee_experience), [user experience](#user_experience)
-   Related article: [**Service Design 101**](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/service-design-101/)

### User Experience (UX)

**User experience (UX)** has two meanings. User experience as a discipline is focused on designing and enhancing the interactions and overall experience for all users of a product, service, or brand; it includes UX research, UX design, content design, information architecture, and several other specialties in this glossary.

User experience is also traditionally used to refer to the holistic relationship — encompassing perceptions, emotions, and interactions — between a person and a product, service, or organization. UX can exist at different levels: a single interaction, a journey involving multiple interactions, or the person’s overall relationship with the organization over time. Some use the term _user experience_ to refer to the interaction-level experience, and _customer experience_ to refer to the broadest, relationship-level experience.

-   See also: [UX design](#ux_design), [UX research](#ux_research), [customer experience](#customer_experience), [human-computer interaction](#human_computer_interaction), [agent experience](#agent_experience)
-   Related article: [**The Definition of User Experience (UX)**](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/definition-user-experience/)

### User-Interface (UI) Design

**User-interface (UI) design** refers to designing a product's user interface: the set of components (e.g., buttons, clickable links), design elements (e.g., images, icons, headers), and interactions that comprise a product such as a website or application. UI design combines interaction design and visual design. The UI is one part of the user experience: a product can have a well-designed UI and still offer a poor experience if it lacks the content or functionality users need.

-   See also: [user experience](#user_experience), [UX design](#ux_design), [interaction design](#interaction_design), [visual design](#visual_design)
-   Related article: [**What Is User Experience (and What Is It Not)?**](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/what-is-user-experience/)

### UX Design

**UX design** is the design practice within the UX field: designing products and services so that they are usable, useful, and appropriate for users' needs and context. UX designers may do interaction design, information architecture, prototyping, and some research; the exact scope varies by organization. UX design is one part of UX, alongside UX research, content design, and other specialties.

-   See also: [user experience](#user_experience), [UI design](#user_interface_design), [product design](#product_design), [interaction design](#interaction_design)
-   Related article: [**UX Basics: Study Guide**](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-basics-study-guide/)

### UX Research

**UX research** (also called **user research**) is the discipline of studying users' behaviors, needs, and motivations, as well as the context in which they might use a product. It can include actual users of a product or service, as well as prospective users. The goal of user research is to inform product-design decisions based on evidence rather than opinions. UX research uses [qualitative](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/5-qualitative-research-methods/) and [quantitative](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/quantitative-user-research-methods/) methods such as [usability testing](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-testing-101/), [interviews](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/user-interviews/), [field studies](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/field-studies/), [diary studies](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/diary-studies/), [surveys](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/survey-best-practices/), and analytics, and it can be done at any stage of product development. UX research can also be used to refer to a particular research effort or study involving data from human participants.

-   See also: [market research](#market_research), [research operations](#research_operations), [human-computer interaction](#human_computer_interaction)
-   Related article: [**When to Use Which User-Experience Research Methods**](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/which-ux-research-methods/)

### UX Writing

**UX writing** is the practice of writing carefully considered information that addresses people's contexts, needs, and behaviors. In other words, UX writers create clear, concise, and contextually appropriate copy (labels, buttons, error messages, instructions) that guides people through an experience. UX writing is narrower than content design, which also covers how content is structured and presented.

-   See also: [content design](#content_design), [content strategy](#content_strategy), [conversation design](#conversation_design)
-   Related article: [**UX Writing: Study Guide**](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-writing-study-guide/)

### Visual Design

**Visual design** is the discipline of determining how design elements such as line, shape, color, grid, and space go together in a product's interface. When applied well, visual design can increase usability, provoke emotion, and strengthen brand perception; it goes beyond making layouts look aesthetically pleasing. In UX, visual design applies the principles of graphic design to interactive interfaces: visual design determines how elements look, and interaction design determines how they behave.

-   See also: [graphic design](#graphic_design), [UI design](#user_interface_design), [interaction design](#interaction_design), [motion design](#motion_design)
-   Related article: [**Visual Design in UX: Study Guide**](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/visual-design-in-ux-study-guide/)
