It’s late at night, and I’m using Figma Make to type out something that once would have taken me weeks three years ago: “A dashboard for a hypothetical logistics ops team, clean, moody, dense tables, sidebar with filter options.” Enter. It thinks. And then there’s something new on my screen that doesn’t belong to me but, in a way, does, because it’s something I’ve asked for, something I will judge.

I take a seat with the output for a few moments. Some of it is superior to anything I could’ve generated in my initial draft. Other parts of it are that special brand of AI that is competent but soulless, the uniformity that has been slowly creeping into everything available online. I begin to edit the prompt. I then pause and open a tab to find out whether there’s anything written about prompts used in dashboards. I forgot that I should be considering the problem definition and not the dashboard itself.

This is the feeling of designing in today’s world.

We’re expected to be proficient technologically, set high standards for craftsmanship, question what products should be, and operate further up the stack. It can be exhausting. It’s also, I’m convinced, the most exciting design problem of our careers.

Let’s unpack exactly what’s happening here because, after all, giving something a name is the first step toward mastering it.

## Everyone designs now

It’s a line that Herbert Simon wrote in 1969, and that still doesn’t seem any less true today: “Everyone designs who devises courses of action aimed at changing existing situations into preferred ones.” It means something pretty broad: engineers design. Doctors design. Policy makers design. The thinking process involved in creating a chair is no different in nature from prescribing treatment or drafting a sales plan.

For many of our careers, that statement was more or less an abstraction. In reality, there was only one place where design happened: in the interface itself. This was the choke point for the execution of design ideas, a bottleneck sometimes begrudgingly held by designers but certainly giving us power because we were the people who could make things beautiful.

That choke point is breaking down.

PMs are prototyping features over coffee using Lovable. Engineers are designing React components between calls in v0. Founders are deploying entire internal tools that they’ve created without much guidance beyond their vibe, all with Claude. Jared Spool’s famous provocation, “anyone who influences what the design becomes is a designer,” used to sound politically motivated. Now, it sounds like the state of the industry.

This isn’t a threat. It’s just a shift in how the world works, a change that forces us to reconsider the very foundation of what it is that we do. When design is no longer the bottleneck, the questions we’re being asked shift from “can you execute?” to “what do you stand for?” And I think this is the underlying question that all of these four factors try to answer.

## The four pressures, felt all at once

Name them separately, but you never experience them separately. All four land in the same hour.

Technical fluency used to be “It helps if you can read HTML.” Today, technical fluency means being aware of what an MCP (Model Context Protocol) server does, how to treat the issue of machine-readable design tokens, what prompting involves and what it means for your design system if its primary user is an AI agent.

Here is what David Robinson said on the topic in 2026 on Medium: “Design systems are no longer built for designers. They’re built for AI. If your tokens aren’t machine-readable, if your component logic isn’t documented in a way that an LLM can parse, you’re leaving performance on the table.” A year ago, this statement would have sounded like a fringe theory.

Not anymore.

Your craft bar continues to rise because the floor level rises as well. AI renders everything “good enough” essentially for free. Each landing page, each dashboard, each empty state appears in the world at the threshold of decent proficiency. That threshold is now so low that anything decent is considered good enough. Dieter Rams talked about “an impenetrable confusion of forms, colours, and noises” already back in 1970, and his advice to deal with this confusion, weniger, aber besser, less, but better, has been about creating distinct products against the background of industrial-age uniformity.

Today, we face a similar challenge with AI-age uniformity. As Source, the Paris design studio, put it in their 2026 trends article: “AI makes everything ‘good enough,’ but good is no longer enough.” Your value-added is becoming increasingly defined by something that a model cannot do: intentionally weird human touch.

I find it especially difficult to explain the importance of rethinking the concept of products to anyone outside design. Back in the day, a product was an obvious construct, a UI element, a visual design flow or a conversion funnel. Now, Maeda suggests thinking about UX in terms of AX or agent experience, which implies that users interact with agents, bypassing about 80 to 90% of the interface, which connects to the product directly. As Source puts it: “Software is no longer consumed, it’s composed.” People are creating their personal software solutions within Claude-created artefacts called Wabi or chatbots called ChatGPT. Our task is not limited to designing products anymore. We now need to create conditions for composing products, summoning them, mediating, and even bypassing them.

Working at a higher level of abstraction implies changing the deliverables. Instead of producing Figma files, we are now supposed to deliver decisions, problem frames, roadmaps, and even playbooks for the governance of a freshly shipped AI feature.

In 2025, the Autodesk AI Jobs Report stated that design skills have become the most demanded ones in job postings related to AI development. And while my interpretation of the situation may differ from yours, personally, I take it as another indication of a shift towards cheap execution and expensive judgment. And you are expected to be equally fluent in both.

None of these things by themselves is a challenge. But doing all four things at once is. Being more technical AND more craft-focused AND more strategic AND more systemic, and voicing your opinion on each of these aspects of the process by Thursday’s design review is the reality today.

## The honest part: this is tiring

I want to state it plainly, because I do not believe that the discourse is doing it justice. Either it is breathless (“the future of design is awesome!”) or doom (“junior designers are done for”), but the truth between those two positions is a little quieter.

Figma designer Ryan Mather said something earlier this year that I keep coming back to: “The word vibe is not right. Vibe coding implies you’re steering it and evaluating it based on vibes, but in practice it’s more like QA whack-a-mole.” His colleague Jake Albaugh put it even more bluntly: “If you know what you want to build and you care about exactly how to get there, it’s not a vibe. It’s a nightmare.” It certainly feels that way to me anytime I’ve sat through hours of Figma Make or Cursor sessions trying to figure out how to coax an interaction from Figma, hitting the prompt again and again in four different ways until finally the model says, yes, it has made your change even though you see nothing on the screen to prove it.

Then there is the issue of tool churn, which goes uncommented on entirely. At least once a quarter, there’s a new paradigm to learn: MCP, design tokens as JSON, make kits, code connect, new models, and now the latest paradigm shift in how your design system is expected to be consumed. None of this is bad in itself; most of us are happy to learn. What is difficult is feeling unceasingly behind the curve while also trying to produce craft-level work.

Finally, there is the issue of personal identity. For those of us who were trained as visual designers and have made our careers with our eyes, it can be discouraging sitting in meetings about which Python libraries you should use to run a prototype. It is not that you cannot learn how to do this; it is just that the nature of the work keeps changing, and you know you will always be sprinting just to catch up.

This is a real thing, and it needs to be stated. Those who pretend otherwise are selling you a course.

## Why this is the best time to be a designer anyway

Here is the part I actually believe.

Every generation of designers has had its own moment like this. The arrival of desktop publishing in the 1980s led, according to legend, to Paul Rand’s famous statement: “over my dead body”. Of course, the man was right to be outraged; the craft that he dedicated his life to would be turned into a remix by people who did not have his principles. The designers who defined the future were not the ones who made such statements, however. Susan Kare took that new pixel grid and turned it into art. The type designers at Emigre managed to create a whole new vocabulary despite the ugliness of the tools. This is the type of people we hear from.

We are living in that Susan Kare moment now. Our tools look ugly and new, and, frankly speaking, some of what is done with their help is indeed bad. Yet, the designer who is ready to go through this pain of seeing their work become something they did not want and who can both refuse to accept that something and keep their sense of taste regardless, will be able to define what the next decade of products actually feels like.

This is not a small accomplishment.

What we get in return, unlike any previous generation, is the ability to design an interface not with something abstract or hypothetical but with human intelligence itself. I mean it in a very practical sense: how do we make a human being trust the decisions made by an algorithm on their behalf? How do we create a sense of ownership when, essentially, the system created all those elements for me? Those are real questions; they are open, and our discipline is prepared to answer them.

Even more important, perhaps, are the things which the current technology removed from our plate, those pixel-perfect exports. The annoying task of renaming all the components. The endless rounds with the colour scheme. We are given the luxury of choosing only good ones.

## What I think actually matters now

Not because of the desire not to end up in a list of prescriptions, the situation is way too dynamic, and whoever says they know how it should be done is bluffing. Here are my attempts to make those moves and bets.

Taste becomes the most valuable asset. Once everything produced can potentially be a masterpiece, judgment becomes the only competitive advantage. Successful designers won’t be those working at the speed of prompts. They will be people knowing which of the six AI-generated variations is the best and are able to explain it in one sentence.

The framing of the problem becomes more important than its execution. Maeda made a point that recently resonated with me: designing things was the second-hardest task; framing the problem is the hardest. Execution was cheap enough, and the skill that became valuable is selecting the right problem.

View your design systems as a product for two customers, humans and AI. This may sound abstract, but it actually isn’t. If the tokens aren’t machine-readable, the prompts produce a generic output and your workflows are broken. The design system is becoming an intelligence layer and, thus, needs to be treated as a product rather than some files you put together.

Use prototyping as a way to think. Prototypes help test assumptions. Vibe coding is terrible for shipping, but absolutely perfect for thinking. Maeda calls it “a blessing for the designer of a hard thing”, being able to prove the existence of an idea in code within an hour of it appearing in your head. That doesn’t make your life easier in terms of production. Instead, it helps you quickly evaluate ideas’ viability.

Orchestration over production. Increasingly, the role will shift from producing artefacts to orchestrating across models, tools, engineers, product managers, and your own judgment. It’s going to be incredibly hard work, but also the least replaceable thing we do.

## Closing

It’s after midnight now. My dashboard looks passable and completely “unfeeling”. I shut the laptop. Tomorrow I will open it again, no doubt in some software that hasn’t even been invented in six months, working on a challenge I haven’t even learned how to articulate yet, all under four stresses that aren’t showing any sign of letting up.

What keeps coming back to me is how the designers who saw desktop publishing and said over my dead body did not get to define what followed. Those who remained curious, slightly weary, refusing to compromise their expectations but equally refusing to hold on to outdated ones, are who we remember.

I believe this is more than anyone has ever asked of us in one breath. And, in a strange way, that’s precisely why it makes sense to stay.
