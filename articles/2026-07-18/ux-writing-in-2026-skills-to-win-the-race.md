_On the current state of the job market_

The tech industry changes fast, and the field of UX is changing even faster. Along with it, the skills you need to bring as a UX Writer are changing, too. Over the past months, I have noticed how the role of the UX Writer shifted tremendously, which is why I put together this overview, based on both my observations and 38 up-to-date job ads for UX Writers and content designers across several countries.

Here’s which skills matter in 2026.

## The role is becoming more strategic

The most consistent pattern in the job ads is that the UX Writer role today is far more strategic than it was a few years ago. A few years ago, the work was mostly about writing the literal words, the actual text elements, and gathering the information needed to make good writing decisions.

Today, the work starts earlier, with the bigger picture of what a product is for within the overall company strategy and how a UX Writer can help the product serve that purpose.

From there, much of the job is defining the communication strategy inside the product, shaping the storyline, and turning all of it into detailed principles, guidelines, and guardrails. The writing itself is then done either by the UX Writer or by AI.

Because of this, the role now consists mainly of defining strategy, creating guidelines, and running quality assurance. Or differently put: the role is changing from an operational one to a strategic one, and that change brings demand for a specific set of skills.

## Professional writing skills are still required

However, the first thing we see in job ads is that strong writing skills are still required, and there is no way around that. Even senior positions ask candidates to prove their writing, and the head of copy and content role at Anthropic is one example where applicants have to demonstrate it.

This makes sense, because both quality assurance and briefing AI systems depend on the ability to write well. The job ads I reviewed reflect this consistently. The skill in question is professional writing in the specific field, plus neighbouring writing skills, and not the "I have loved writing since I was a teenager" kind.

## Brand storytelling and voice and tone guidelines

A newer requirement is brand storytelling, together with the ability to translate that storytelling into voice and tone guidelines that both human writers and AI can use. This has always mattered to some degree, but the emphasis on it is growing. T

his growing emphasis follows from the flood of digital products entering the market, many of them AI-generated and written in generic, interchangeable language, sometimes called AI slop. Against that background, a distinct and well-applied brand voice becomes an essential competitive advantage rather than a nice-to-have.

This work requires understanding business and brand strategy, in some cases helping to shape it, and then deriving the implications for the brand voice and designing guidelines that people can actually apply. In my own work, refining and adding to our brand voice guidelines, and acting as the protector and advocate for our voice and tone, is one of the most important day-to-day tasks.

## AI literacy

AI literacy is among the top mentions in the job ads. In the context of UX Writing, it means knowing how the most common tools work, such as ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini, and knowing how to use them specifically for product copy and related tasks.

It also means knowing how to structure and phrase prompts, how to brief and train an agent, how to build proper briefing materials, and where these tools are strong or weak for UX Writing. More advanced cases exist, such as linking Claude to Figma and automating the application of entire content design systems, but how far this goes depends on the company and the product.

## Content design systems and scalable patterns

Closely related is the ability to build and use content design systems and scalable patterns. This was not as important one or two years ago, but companies now expect UX Writers to combine these systems with AI to automate content production at scale.

The process runs in several steps, from establishing the rules and building the basics to setting up, testing, running, and continuously improving the system, which then allows you to automate content production. Companies are highly interested in this, so experience with it, or at least the curiosity and willingness to experiment, is in demand. This is likely to become more important in the coming years.

## Research and data analysis

Research and data analysis also appear among the top three mentions. The expectation is – more than ever – that copy and brand decisions no longer come from intuition or gut feeling, but from user data that is used to explain and test them. UX Writers are now often asked to participate in the conceptualization and even conducting of interviews, to be literate in basic data analysis, to run A/B tests, and to derive decisions from the results.

Experience with this in a portfolio or CV is a strong advantage, because the days of answering "I think it is the most empathetic way to say it" are over. In my own work, I take part in user and customer research often, and the team looks at numbers and statistics regularly.

As the person responsible for our entire written customer communication, I also work with conversion rates, newsletter and email open rates, and heat maps that show where people scroll on our website. Qualitative and quantitative insights therefore play a major part in the writing work I do.

## Accessibility and inclusion

Accessibility and inclusion are becoming more important for two reasons. First, they are now a legal requirement in many countries. Second, AI is notoriously weak at handling them, which makes careful briefings, guidelines, and thorough quality assurance necessary. This requires knowledge of accessibility and inclusion standards and how to apply them to UX copy, information architecture, and even brand voice and communication guidelines.

## Collaboration

Collaboration skills are old but still essential, and they rank among the top five in the job ads. The work involves navigating networks of stakeholders, customers, management, and team members, and knowing where and how to involve each of them. It also involves doing this smoothly, so that their interests, restrictions, and limitations are covered and the resulting product copy aligns with what they need. This skill is still important with or without AI.

## The bigger picture: upsides and downsides

Taking a broader perspective, we see that on the positive side, the work becomes more interesting and more powerful in terms of product and process impact, and the role overlaps more with neighbouring disciplines such as marketing, brand strategy, and product management. As a result, anyone who later moves out of UX Writing can bring a set of transferable skills that make such a move easier.

On the negative side, the role also becomes more complex and more demanding, because it now asks for a wider and more varied set of skills. Many of these skills come with experience, which makes the job market harder for juniors and beginners. For a beginner, this can mean that landing a job now requires a different approach than it would have a couple of years ago.

Taken together, these requirements mean the scope of UX Writing is broadening. Where the job was mostly about words and phrasing a few years ago, it now includes technical integration, automation, communication strategy, and brand strategy – an exciting trend that comes with its own challenges and chances.
