> _tl;dr: Organisations say they want innovation but keep killing it. The problem is that the organisational decision-making machinery assumes you already know enough to justify a big bet, and genuinely new things never cross that bar. Big visible bets also trigger a predictable organisational immune response that neutralises, ejects, or quarantines anything new and unfamiliar. The way through is to make new things small, boring, and apparently routine, so they don’t set off the immune system in the first place._
> 
> _Adapted from my keynote at the [Public Innovation Lab Congress](https://www.congresodesafiospublicos.cl/), Santiago, June 2026._

Japanese has a character, 青, that is used to refer to [both “blue” and “green”](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue%E2%80%93green_distinction_in_language): 青い空 is “blue sky,” 青草 is “green grass.” Now imagine a typical scenario where the protagonist in the action movie is defusing a bomb with the help of their handler in a faraway situation room: “The instructions say to cut the “青” wire to shut off the timer.” When one word covers two different things, dangerous mistakes can happen.

We do this with the word “risk,” [using it for almost every kind of not-knowing](https://vaughntan.org/overloading-and-appropriation) about the future. Hiding under that one word are [two fundamentally different kinds of unknown](https://vaughntan.org/uncertaintyrisk).

**Risk** is a known game. You know the moves you can make, the outcomes that can result, the odds, and what each outcome is worth (this is [Frank Knight’s distinction, from 1921](https://www.econlib.org/library/Knight/knRUP.html)). Risk is calculable, optimisable, insurable. You can learn about it from the past. Risk is reassuring.

**Uncertainty** is a fundamentally different kind of unknown, not just a bigger risk. Uncertainty consists of not-knowing about [the actions you can take and the outcomes that might result](https://vaughntan.org/not-knowing-about-actions-and-outcomes), the [causation](https://vaughntan.org/causal-not-knowing) connecting them, or their [relative subjective value](https://vaughntan.org/not-knowing-about-value). Uncertainty is unquantifiable and uninsurable. The past can’t teach you about uncertainty. Uncertainty is uncomfortable.

The discomfort hides something: uncertainty is both threat and opportunity. Every genuinely new thing started in a moment where no one knew the possible actions, the outcomes, or how the result would be valued. A new vaccine in months instead of years. Letting women vote for the first time. Each looked at the time like dangerous uncertainty, but it was also productive uncertainty. For innovation, [uncertainty is a feature, not a bug](https://vaughntan.org/innovation-and-not-knowing): by definition, new things are only possible when there is uncertainty about actions, outcomes, causation, or value.

## Decision-making machinery built for risk

Innovation is uncertainty work, but most organisations only know how to do risk work.

Think back to the last time your organisation faced something it genuinely did not understand. You already know the reflex: “Let’s do a cost-benefit analysis,” or “We should think about the expected value of doing it this way.” The people in organisations reach for these risk-oriented decision-making tools instinctively.

The World Health Organisation did this in February 2020 when it recommended [not restricting travel or trade based on “a careful risk assessment”](https://web.archive.org/web/20200308003518/https://www.who.int/news-room/articles-detail/updated-who-recommendations-for-international-traffic-in-relation-to-covid-19-outbreak/) driven by data from the earlier SARS epidemic. The result was rapid global spread of COVID-19, and [a 3.1% contraction in world output](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/weo/issues/2021/10/12/world-economic-outlook-october-2021). Every quantitative and thus “rigorous” decision-making tool organisations use ultimately resolves to expected value or cost-benefit, and every one requires risk assumptions to hold. Used on genuine uncertainty, they [give false confidence, and then they break](https://vaughntan.org/the-insidiousness-of-the-formal-risk-mindset).

The risk mindset and its associated machinery are woven through goals, hiring, and rewards. A target assumes you already know what you are aiming at, so the metric quietly punishes the exploration you asked for. Job descriptions encode responsibilities of well-understood roles, then you ask employees to do something entirely new which their JDs don’t cover. Make failure career-ending, and people cling tightly to the status quo.

The public sector runs the strongest version of this, even though public problems are often wicked problems which most need genuinely new answers. In the public sector, everything is protocolised, coordination across agencies is endless, long-term work is driven by short election cycles, political office-holders demand certainty even about uncertain matters, and everything is embedded in an enormous and long-standing hierarchy. Each of these characteristics of the public sector is understandable, but together they make the public sector the hardest place in which to do the uncertainty work it most needs. Large companies and older foundations run their own versions of the same setup.

## The idea-killer

Bring to mind one genuinely new thing your organisation tried, and then killed off. Not something that quietly ran out of steam: something that got going, got noticed, and got stopped.

It probably didn’t die because it was bad. Your organisation wanted something new, so it did what its machinery does: it made the new thing a big bet. Under uncertainty, big is the wrong move in two ways. A big bet on the new is not bold; it is necessarily blind, because you cannot know enough up front to justify it. And a big, visible bet is just what the parts of an organisation that want to keep things the same will move to get rid of. The better the idea, the bigger the bet you are tempted to make, and the bigger the target you paint on it.

In 2010, [after storms and landslides killed people across Rio de Janeiro](https://centreforpublicimpact.org/public-impact-fundamentals/rio-de-janeiros-centre-of-operations-cor/) due to poor inter-agency coordination, the city built a single situation room where thirty city agencies (police, traffic, weather, civil defence) could watch and work together to respond to the same live data. This innovation worked: emergency responses got faster and better-coordinated. The city political leadership marketed it as a splashy flagship, and showed it off around the world. A flagship like this is one of the first things a new leadership team notices: visible, named, belonging to the people who came before. At the next political transition it was moved out of the secretariat that had built it, and left to drift.

What if the same situation room had been built to be boring and prosaic? No big launch, just procedures baked into how every department already worked. Too boring to put on a hit list when administrations change, it would probably have survived by being overlooked.

## The organisational immune system

Innovation efforts keep dying though everyone says they want innovation. It isn’t stupidity or hostility. Organisations have an immune system, and innovation efforts invariably look like foreign invaders. So the immune system attacks and tries to neutralise, eject, or quarantine them.

**Neutralise:** In 1881, [the Cuban doctor Carlos Finlay](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16435764/) told a conference that yellow fever was carried not by filth or bad air but by one specific mosquito. For nearly twenty years the medical establishment treated the idea as a joke, while people kept dying. When an outside team finally tested it, he was completely right: control the mosquito and the fever vanishes. Havana was cleared in months; [the Panama Canal could finally be built](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8932943/). The unfamiliar good science behind the idea was buried and neutralised by ridicule.

**Eject:** In 1985, [Steve Jobs was forced out of Apple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steve_Jobs), the company he had built: too unfamiliar, too much friction. The body rejected its own founder. (Apple eventually brought him back, and only then became the company you know today.)

**Quarantine:** For two hundred years Japan closed itself to the world but still wanted access to Western knowledge, so it confined Western traders to [Dejima, a small fan-shaped island in Nagasaki harbour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dejima). Everything passed through one gate: a controlled trickle of new ideas. In organisations, quarantine often looks like a lab, an innovation unit, or a special team which is allowed to be weird, from which a controlled trickle of new ideas is permitted.

The idea-killer is an organisational immune system whose job is to keep things stable: a completely predictable and justifiable response. Fighting it harder only provokes a bigger reaction. The fix is to not trigger it in the first place.

## Responsibly sneaky strategies

Resist the temptation to make the innovation into a big and noticeable flagship project. Make it [small, boring, apparently routine](https://vaughntan.org/boringtinytools), so the system never notices it enough to flag it as foreign. Let it grow only as it justifies itself. As it grows, it becomes harder to neutralise, eject, or quarantine.

The moves to make are the undramatic ones. Start small enough that no one sees a threat. Make tests cheap, fast, and numerous, so failure is survivable: a portfolio of small bets each placed to answer useful questions, instead of one big bet placed to create the impression of decisiveness. Disguise the innovation as an unremarkable update to standard procedure. The idea is to not fight the system head-on.

In Buenos Aires, a small United Nations team wanted to know how air pollution really varied across the city. The official way is to put in fixed monitoring stations costing tens of thousands of dollars each. Instead, they [taught students to build air sensors each costing about US$150](https://www.undp.org/acceleratorlabs/smarter-together/using-diy-hardware-and-citizen-sensing-measure-changes-air-quality-argentina), gave twenty volunteers one each, and asked them to carry it on their bikes for seven weeks. No launch, no headline: a cheap experiment below everyone’s notice. It found what the expensive stations had missed — pollution hotspots at traffic junctions, far worse than the city-wide average. Cheap data the official system couldn’t produce, and couldn’t argue with. Not big enough to be a target, and also too useful to ignore. The Rio story inverted.

A small, boring new thing survives by being unnoticeable until it is undeniable.

If this sounds like working behind the organisation’s back, consider what the alternative gambles with. The innovation big bet stakes public money and public trust on a guess, faking certainty about something genuinely uncertain. The small, quiet experiment spends almost nothing to buy real knowledge, and the public is never exposed to a large, irreversible downside. Being responsibly sneaky isn’t cheating. It’s how you take care of public resources in a world you cannot predict.

## Two jobs, depending on where you sit

If you lead an organisation, your job is to calm the organisation immune system. Protect and encourage small experiments. You can do this by introducing protocols (often in the HR and compensation space) for rewarding learning especially when associated with failure, and by making it easy (often through procurement and management approval threshold changes) to fund many small things instead of a few big things. Most of it is organisational design work: tuning goals, rewards, and permissions so that small, low-stakes experiments become easy and unremarkable, and being responsibly sneaky becomes the ordinary way to work.

If you work inside the machine, you already have the playbook. Don’t design and pitch a big and flashy bet. Start small and boring, and let the results argue for you.

Doing something genuinely new is uncertainty work, and you can’t do uncertainty work with one big, certain-looking bet. You do it small and quietly, and you either stay beneath notice or become undeniable. That is how you do new things in organisations built to kill innovation.
