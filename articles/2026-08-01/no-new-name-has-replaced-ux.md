Summary:  A survey of 604 tech and design professionals found that “UX” remains the default name for our field, while alternative names remain fragmented.

Many UX practitioners are questioning how the field should describe itself. Amid UX layoffs, tighter budgets, and ongoing pressure to prove the value of UX work, some have wondered whether the terms “UX” or “user experience” still serve the field well, or whether a new label might better communicate the work’s relevance and impact. To understand which terms resonate, we asked tech and design professionals to name the field in their own words.

## Why the “UX” Label Is Being Reconsidered

Our field has gone through many changes in terminology, driven partly by genuine expansions in scope and partly by [vocabulary inflation](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iGAWbtSxFww). Earlier work was often discussed under the heading of “usability,” which focuses primarily on whether people can use a system effectively and efficiently. “User-centered design” described the accompanying approach to designing systems: grounding design decisions in an understanding of users’ needs, tasks, and contexts, generally through iterative research and evaluation. It was later joined — and sometimes replaced — by “human-centered design.”

 “[User experience](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/definition-user-experience/)” subsequently emerged as a broader concept that also encompassed people’s perceptions, emotions, and overall interactions with an organization or a product. Meanwhile, practitioners from marketing and customer-service backgrounds popularized “[customer experience,”](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-vs-cx/) a related term that was often treated as roughly equivalent to UX despite somewhat different origins and emphases. More recently, many organizations have adopted “design” as an even broader umbrella, while others use “experience design” — sometimes to signal a scope extending beyond individual interfaces or products. These terms have not simply replaced one another; they continue to coexist, with considerable ambiguity and overlap.

At the same time, the boundaries around UX are becoming less distinct. [Product managers](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/product-manager-archetype/) are increasingly involved in research and design work. [Research democratization](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/democratize-user-research/) has already expanded who participates in user research, and AI tools are accelerating that trend by making it easier for people outside traditional UX roles to draft [research plans](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/plan-research-ai/), [generate interface ideas](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ai-prototyping/), [analyze feedback](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/research-with-ai/), and [create design artifacts](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/common-ux-deliverables/). We’ve also seen how AI has contributed to [the return of the UX generalist](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/return-ux-generalist/).

These shifts raise a practical question: **when professionals describe a human-centered approach to designing products and services, what language do they use?** Are practitioners moving away from “UX” toward terms like “product,” “experience design,” or “human-centered design”? Or does “UX” still remain the default label?

We conducted a research study to understand what terms tech and design professionals currently use to describe this field.

## How We Studied UX Terminology

In April 2026, we surveyed 604 tech and design professionals worldwide through NN/G’s Practitioner Panel, social channels, LinkedIn, Instagram, and newsletter.

We deliberately asked the survey question indirectly. Rather than asking, “What terms do you use to define our field?” (which could invite people to repeat a trendy label), we showed participants a definition of UX without mentioning the term. Then, we asked them to name the field or discipline that the definition described. This approach was designed to surface the relevant term that came to mind unprompted, rather than the one people thought they should say.

> **Survey Question:** Which professional field or discipline do you believe best describes the following work?
> 
> _An individual gathers insights about people’s needs and experiences and turns those insights into recommendations for how a product or service should be planned, built, and improved._
> 
> Please name only 1 field or discipline.

Responses written as job titles were standardized by converting them to the corresponding field or discipline. For example, “UX researcher” was recoded as “UX research”. No other changes were made to the wording of responses. Also, because some participants included more than one label in their answer, responses were analyzed based on whether they contained specific keywords rather than being assigned to a single mutually exclusive category.

This study had limitations. It was not geographically representative of all regions because some regions had small sample sizes. In addition, our recruitment channels likely reached people with prior exposure to NN/G materials, which tend to favor the term “UX.” These participants may not be representative of the entire population of UX professionals.

## “UX” Is the Most Common Term

“UX” was by far the most common individual label, appearing in 47.8% of responses. Altogether, **70% included terminology associated with UX**, such as “UX” (47.8% of responses), “user” (22.4% of the responses) or “user experience” (10.8% of responses).

No competing label came close.

Terms proposed as alternatives for defining this work were strikingly rare:

-   Only 4 responses included “experience design.”
-   Only 5 responses included the word “human” (as in “human-centered design”).

### Alternative Labels Remain Fragmented

Responses that didn’t include UX-related keywords used a wide breadth of different labels, including “consumer-behavior research,” “digital-experience strategy,” “journey management,” “service-design research,” and “human-centered design research.”

Thus, alternative labels remain fragmented. This finding further indicates that **no alternative term has emerged as a broadly shared replacement for “UX.”**

Alternative labels used to describe UX work widely varied. Examples include: “product management,” “customer-experience research,” and “consumer-behavior research.”

### Job Roles May Shape the Terminology People Use

The terminology that participants chose to respond with may be influenced by their job role.

For example, 12% of study participants used “product” in their response. Segmenting this finding by job role reveals an interesting pattern: 38% of product managers and 27% of product designers used “product” in their response, compared with only 10% of UX practitioners. This difference was statistically significant.

This pattern suggests that the use of “product” appears to be concentrated among people whose job roles already include the term. Product managers and product designers may describe the work through the language of their roles, particularly as they become more involved in UX activities.

Another example comes from the 2.65% of participants who used “customer” in their response. 18.18% of customer insights professionals used “customer”, compared with only 0.50% of UX practitioners. This difference was statistically significant.

## Keep “UX” as the Umbrella Term (for Now)

Based on our findings, **adopting a new umbrella term for the field does not seem warranted at this point.** When participants described the work in their own words, UX-related terminology was by far the most common. No alternative label — including “product,” “experience design,” or “human-centered design” — showed comparable spontaneous recognition across the sample.

This does not conclusively mean that “UX” is the clearest possible term or that another label doesn’t have the potential to communicate the work more effectively. Our study measured which terminology came to mind, rather than how well each term conveyed the field’s scope, value, or responsibilities. Further, our findings found that someone’s own job role may influence what label they would assign to this work. **Determining whether a different label would improve understanding of the field would require a separate study that directly compared how people interpret and respond to different terms.**

For now, UX leaders should therefore focus less on finding a better umbrella term and more on [making the contribution of UX visible](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2pou-FnTaFM). That means connecting UX work to the decisions, outcomes, and risks that stakeholders already care about. Leaders can show how human-centered work reduces uncertainty, uncovers unmet customer needs, prevents costly rework, identifies accessibility problems, and improves the quality of what teams ship. 

### Conclusion

Despite ongoing discussion about renaming the field, “UX” remains the most widely recognized name for the field among the professionals in our sample. Although respondents used many alternative labels, the alternatives remained fragmented and possibly influenced by participants’ job roles. This finding does not prove that “UX” is the clearest possible term for our work, but it does suggest that renaming the discipline is unlikely to solve its current challenges. For now, practitioners would be better served by communicating what UX work contributes, which decisions it improves, and what risks it helps organizations avoid.
