As a teenager I spent my pocket money on Pantone Tria alcohol markers. They were very expensive. I checked: with inflation adjustment, about €12 each. Over the years, I collected enough to fill 3 boxes with 12 each. They’ve proven their worth, because I still use them.

Some of them still look almost new! The only thing I regret is that I thought COPIC markers looked lame (I mean, I still think they do, next to the others).

Tria Pantone markers were produced by Letraset, which main business whose rub-on lettering sheets. I guess that business was already declining when I bought my first markers and in 2012 the brand was sold to Winsor & Newton.

Now, even my 30 year-old markers are still in great condition, but the whole product line has been discontinued. Instead, Winsor and Newton now offers the Promarker line. They’re a much cheaper design with a similar shape. In a set, they cost less than €3 each. But:

-   They don’t have the (mostly useless) third nib that gave Tria markers their name
-   There are fewer colors
-   Only the broad nib is removable, no replacements are offered
-   There are no Promarker refills

I guess they thought, at that price no professional would want to spend time with refilling and replacing nibs. I do, though. I don’t use these markers professionally, I don’t like waste and I don’t mind taking care of my stuff. Maybe, after all these years, I just experience a bit of [tsukumogami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsukumogami) too. Moreover, having a refill bottle allows me to control flow better. And with an ink bottle you can do er … special effects, like blotches and whatnot.

Luckily COPIC is still around. With a bit of trying, I found matching COPIC refills for my Promarkers. For instance, I found that Promarker Meadow Green is basically COPIC YG11 and Promarker Blue is close to COPIC BG72.

And this is how I started to love COPIC. I first bought some refills, but when I attempted to remove the brush nib of my Meadow Green marker, it broke off. So just to try it, I bought a few COPIC Ciao and COPIC Sketch markers.

They really are superior to Promarkers (and Tria Markers, for that matter). The ink and nibs are as good as Trias’. However, COPIC colors are not printed on the marker labels but molded into the caps. So their colors don’t dissolve with messy refilling! Of course, the colors don’t match as accurately with the inks as the labels did with the ink in Pantone markers, but that ship has sailed. Also, while (strangely, only some) of my Pantone labels have faded beyond recognition, the colors on COPIC caps seem to be more stable. Moreover, there’s a system in COPIC’s color codes that can help you to mix colors better (I’ll get back to that later).

I really like the COPIC Ciao series. At €3–5, they’re more expensive than Promarkers but cheaper than the classic COPIC Sketch markers. They’re thin and light, which makes them easy to handle. I love that they take up only little space when I’m traveling. Sure, they contain less ink than the bigger markers, but since I buy refills for the colors I use a lot anyway, that doesn’t matter.

The white marker with the handwritten 4 is a Pantone Cool Gray 4, the one color that all Industrial Design Engineering students from TU Delft used for everything. After the countless messy refills, the label print has dissolved. In reality, Rubine Red looks much more magenta, but I didn’t want to spend more time improving the photo, as the other colors do look realistic.

## Tips for picking marker colors

When I started out, I just picked the colors I _liked_ most. I still love Pantone 246 and Rubine Red. When I see them, I want to eat them! I haven’t used these markers very often, though. I never even refilled 246 after 3 decades.

What I’m saying is, a color that’s nice to see may not be the most versatile.

I’m attracted to 246 and Rubine Red because they’re so saturated. They really stand out. But when you color something, not everything should stand out, so you can only use such colors sparingly.

Here are some tips to avoid buying what only looks good on the shelf.

### Tip 1: Start a set with light colors

Now this depends on what style you’re after, but generally I think it’s best to start with very light markers.

Here’s why:

1.  You can layer ink when you need a darker variant
2.  With light colors, it’s easier to let our brain correct what would be considered mistakes. A face shaded with some very light blue will look like a shaded face. The same with a medium dark color will look like an intentionally blue face.
3.  When you mix two or more colors, the result will be darker than the starting colors. After all, each color lets the paper absorb more light.
4.  With one light gray, you can add shadow to each color.

Three to five light colors already form a versatile watercolor-like palette.

If you’re into COPIC, that means color codes with the numbers 11, 00 or even 000. The 0000 range is too light for a starter set. In fact, I find Y000 is almost imperceptible(sidenote:Write me if you live in Germany and want my COPIC Ciao Y000! ) already.

### Tip 2: Decide what colors you _need_

That may sound obvious, but I still get tempted buy these nice sets with predefined color palettes. They usually come with an example illustration where all those colors do nicely combine. But that’s never something I want to draw myself.

Lately I’m really into making quick street and landscape scenes. It would be cool to do that with [watercolors](https://www.kooslooijesteijn.net/blog/i-like-watercolor-painting), but I still kind of suck at that. Especially outside and on the train, markers are a lot more convenient. For that, I only bring a few colors. One for skies, one for foliage, one for objects. This has been serving me well, but I noticed that having only one color for skies and water is a limitation that is somewhat unnecessary. Also, it’d be nice to have a darker green for shading (the magic potion greens I’ve got already are not suitable for that).

### Tip 3: Don’t buy too much

Sometimes I think I should just get a big set of Ohuhu markers. In such a set, markers cost less than €1 each. I’d have a set so big I could only have dreamed of it as a teenager! But I already know I’m not going to use most of them anyway. They’ll get in my way, confuse, me and I will find it too wasteful to throw the unused markers away. After all, I still have Pantone 246 from 1997.

Despite having 36 markers at the time, even in all my years as a design student, I rarely used any other than my 7 favorites. Each color had a specific purpose and I knew exactly how each reacted to the others.

The other day, I spent two hours comparing just some of the marker colors I have now (42). I actually enjoyed doing that. But it was a good reminder that the color on the outside of a marker is rarely a perfect indicator of what the ink looks like on paper. You really have to remember how they look and, just as importantly, how they mix with the other colors. As the number of possible combinations increases exponentially with each marker you add, I really recommend keeping the number as small as possible and not buying new colors more than once a month.

### Tip 4: Create your own color wheels

You can’t really trust digital versions; you really need to see what your ink looks like on your paper.

Keep in mind that we typically assess colors by three variables: hue, saturation, and lightness. You can’t make a two-dimensional color wheel showing all three. If you have a lot of colors, you’re probably best off making multiple color wheels with one level of saturation for each.

And remember that brown is just dark orange.

### Tip 5: Create a chart with color mixes

Initially, I loved alcohol markers for the print-like, even surfaces you can make with them. But now I find it just as important that they can be used to mix colors really well.

This requires practice, and the easiest way to do it is just to occasionally make a big chart with all the colors you’d ever want to mix. (That doesn’t mean _all_ colors. I never mix a very light with a very dark color, as the result would be indistinguishable from the dark color.)

### Tip 6: Create your own palettes

Some colors just go well together, where others maybe not so much. To develop and mix palettes for different purposes can be a relaxing, semi-creative exercise after a busy workday. And the results help when you want to quickly grab a few colors to color in a sketch.

### Tip 6: Understand the color naming system of your ink

It took me way too long to appreciate COPIC’s color coding system. For two decades, it was my own ignorance, but it’s also because they apply the system inconsistently and don’t explain it very clearly.

This is how I interpret the [COPIC color coding system](https://copicmarkers.com/blogs/educational/copic-color-system#color):

Part

Meaning, official

Meaning, in reality

Letter(s)

Color family

Hue. Except for grays (C, N, T, W), achromatic (A) and fluorescent (F).

First number (0–9)

Blending group, refers to color saturation

Higher numbers have more gray added, making the color darker and less saturated. But! Within color families, it can also indicate a slightly different hue.

Subsequent numbers (000–9)

Intensity value aka brightness number

Darkness: higher numbers are darker without loosing saturation.

So ‘BG72’ means ‘blue-green with quite a lot of gray, but not very dark.’ ‘YG000’ means ‘yellow-green, saturated and very, very light.’

Most COPIC colors only have two numbers. I’ve only seen additional zeroes for very light to extremely light colors. I guess they added these only after the two-number system was established.

Now all this is useful because the codes help predict:

1.  What colors go well together because they share or have contrasting properties. If all properties are completely different, it’s unlikely the colors go well together. Colors from different color families but with the same numbers often go well together.
2.  What colors mix well. If the first number and the subsequent numbers from one color differ only two or less from another color, you can probably blend them nicely without an obvious transition.
3.  What colors can cover other colors. If the numbers are 6 or more apart, it’s likely that the lighter color barely makes a change to the darker color.

### Tip 7: Use marker paper for testing

When you’re comparing colors and are deciding between, for instance, a very light or the lightest blue, the paper may make as big a difference as the ink itself.

There’s nothing really wrong with using regular paper with markers, but the colors may appear a bit more dull. Marker paper sucks up less ink, and that makes the colors look more brilliant. And that behavior alone is worth the extra costs anyway, even (or perhaps, especially) when you’re just mucking around with colors and not even making something finished.

### Tip 8: Buy from an actual store

You can probably buy markers a bit cheaper online, but with the added shipping costs, it may come down to the same price as at your local art supplies store. These places are the best! And that alone is worth spending a few bucks on. And now I’ve learned that a refillable marker with replaceable nibs literally lasts a lifetime, and I’m not buying many anyway, it’s really not an expensive hobby.

Anyway, you may pay less for a good brand at a real store than when you buy cheap stuff online. The cheap brands only offer big sets. In the store, you can actually try the colors you consider. Due to how colors are saved and rendered on computer displays, online charts really aren’t great for finding the exact colors you’re looking for.

### Tip 9: Remember there’s no vendor lock-in

As mentioned, I’ve filled up my Promarkers with COPIC inks. You can buy an empty (!) Ciao marker and fill it with ink from the Sketch line or a different brand altogether. Or fill a used, dried-out marker with ink of a similar color. Some people even like to mix their own inks. Or buy and revive second-hand markers.

My marker pouch looks a bit messy with four types of markers, but I’ve started to like that, as the different shapes of the markers make it easier to find the right color.

## Why even bother with markers in 2026!?

If markers require so much mental overhead and each color brings additional costs, why even bother and not just do everything digitally, I often asked myself. Well:

1.  Computer displays have become good, but there are still color nuances you can only see with good ink (or paint) on paper. For a fraction of the costs of a good display.
2.  All my creative skills are in some way digital now. Some are completely digital, meaning after a day of work at the computer, I again sit down at the computer writing, editing photos, or making music. That gets boring.
3.  Immediate, tangible output of my creations is nice. I rarely finish a drawing I’d find worth printing, but altogether my sketches are something I enjoy flipping through much more than a folder on my computer with JPEGs.
4.  You can use markers everywhere, even where the sun’s bright and when the battery of your tablet is empty.
5.  Sometimes people want to see what I’ve made, and they can then take a look at my sketchbook in a way that’s much nicer than with any digital device.
6.  Even a shitty sketch can make a good gift (I rarely give them away because I don’t think they’re very good, but I love every handmade creation I’ve ever received myself).
7.  A digital creation is never finished, but with ink on paper, every drawing has a point where if you add ink, it can only get worse.

The whole [reason I got into design](https://www.kooslooijesteijn.net/en/design-and-art) was that I like drawing. I don’t use markers as much as at Industrial Design Engineering. But even in UI design, I find pen-and-paper sketching [absolutely essential](https://www.kooslooijesteijn.net/blog/free-hand-sketches-in-figma) because they don’t have most of the constraints that digital tools have.
