##### November 2026

I’m tired. It’s my own fault, I’m working long hours — but not because anyone has asked me to, I’m actually having quite a bit of fun.

This all reminds me of an old creative industry diagram I read on Kottke in 2005, back when I was a junior designer: [Good, Fast, Cheap — Pick Two](https://kottke.org/05/04/pick-two). The idea was that with creative work, you could have any two of the _good, fast, cheap_ trifecta… but never all three. Cheap and fast wouldn’t be good, good and cheap wouldn’t be fast, and good and fast wouldn’t be cheap.

As a smartass junior I thought I was very clever wheeling this out to any PM who asked me for a rush job.

Of course, throughout my early career I was regularly forced to provide all three when a client or manager hit me with a same-day deadline, usually on a Friday. In those cases it **was** possible to deliver good, fast and cheap — but it came at the cost of my own energy. There was a personal cost which was the fourth, hidden variable in that diagram nobody talked about.

Cut to 2026. AI has made good work fast and cheap. Now we _can_ have all three… I’m genuinely producing the best work of my career, and I’m having fun doing it. But I’m working long hours, and there’s something familiar about that personal cost from 20 years ago. I’m not alone, either. I speak to designers across the industry regularly, and this seems to be the new normal. I’ll bet you’re feeling it too.

Recent [research from UC Berkeley](https://www.universityofcalifornia.edu/news/ai-promised-free-workers-time-uc-berkeley-haas-researchers-found-opposite) describes how, despite being able to do more in less time, people using AI are taking on larger projects, filling expertise gaps and intensifying their workloads in potentially unsustainable ways. [Zach Chmael describes a similar experience](https://substack.com/home/post/p-213133485) in a way that resonated with me, noting that AI has increased his capability but not his capacity:

“AI does not only help me finish work. It makes **more work** feel possible.”

I feel this every time I go to finish work, but then decide to get Claude to make one extra tiny improvement just because I can. And then 2 hours pass…

## The cost is coherence[Direct link](#cost-is-coherence "Link to this section")

The research does a good job of explaining why I keep finding more work to do. But it doesn’t fully explain why I feel increasingly compelled to stay connected to everyone else’s work. It’s not just my own productivity that’s accelerating. It’s everyone else’s, too. This came up in a recent interview with [Case Study Club](https://www.casestudy.club/buzz-usborne-interview) when I blurted out:

“AI is a chaos machine.”

I was being extreme, for dramatic effect. But the sentiment behind it is that every new thing we quickly throw together with best intentions introduces possibilities, interactions and dependencies we can’t fully anticipate. A new requirement, a bug, a new mental model, component or an entirely different type of use. It’s not always bad, but it’s rarely planned — and often that trends towards chaos.

This has always been the case, of course. Complexity is the burden product teams have always had to actively manage, because left unattended it threatens the overall coherence of a product. But now it’s happening… _faster_.

Now with AI, each person on a team can realistically create 10 meaningful changes per week. Those ten might bring ten exciting new capabilities… but they might also introduce ten different ways of working and ten new things for customers to learn. They create new objects to maintain, document and support. New interactions with existing features, new expectations and potentially new contradictions. Without a shared direction, each improvement risks making the product as a whole feel less coherent.

**That’s** what’s compelling me to stay way more connected to the work than ever before!

Even my “what if” pitches carry a different weight now. I used to be able to pitch an ambitious vision of where a product might be in a year’s time, knowing that plenty of the details were illustrative, unresolved or simply made up on the spot to tell a compelling story. The point was to build alignment around a direction, not provide a blueprint.

Now, there’s the very real risk of someone taking a single frame from that pitch and turning it into a working feature before we’ve even considered how it fits into the rest of the product. Or whether it should exist at all. What was intended as inspiration can suddenly become implementation. More chaos.

This is the fourth variable at play. Good, fast and cheap work has come at the cost of **coherence**. How everything fits together.

AI has made it cheaper for individual teams to move quickly. But it’s increased the cost of ensuring they’re moving in the same direction. We’ve democratised the ability to build things much faster than we’ve democratised the ability to understand how those things fit together. I suspect that cost is increasingly being absorbed by anyone trying to make sense of it all. It’s exhausting.

Catt Small, Dropbox’s Staff Designer [talked at Config ‘26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WGZ38oeoBtU) about the risk of teams moving incredibly fast in the wrong direction. I think this might be what she was getting at. And it’s not even the “wrong” direction — it’s just not the _same_ direction.

## Keeping up[Direct link](#keeping-up "Link to this section")

For me, it’s maintaining coherence that’s causing the long days. I’m not just generating more work for myself (although I am doing that, and I’m loving it. Sue me) — but I’m also responding to the great work that everyone else is generating. I used to have a pretty good understanding of what other teams were working on just because timelines were long enough for me to passively pick up any overlap.

In the before-times, what was discussed at the start of the cycle was roughly what was being worked on at the end. Now every day is a surprise!

I’m definitely glad those days are over. But now I find myself opening Slack at 6am, having logged off at 6pm the previous evening, to discover an entire feature has sprung into existence overnight. Can I approve the PR? Maybe. But I haven’t even had a chance to consider how it fits into everything else we’re building. Suddenly, I’m the blocker. So I compensate by staying connected to more people, more decisions and more work, more of the time.

And I suspect leadership teams are feeling this too. From the outside it can look like decisions are changing every five minutes. Priorities shift, features get renamed, directions change, the competition ships. It’s easy to interpret this as indecision, but I wonder whether it’s a symptom of the same problem. People are making decisions with the best information available, only for the landscape around those decisions to change almost immediately. What made sense yesterday is out of date today.

It’s not just the cognitive load of constant reorientation that’s exhausting — it’s also the effort of keeping up with everything that’s changing around us — including keeping the design system up to date. Not just keeping components consistent between Figma and code, but capturing the reasoning behind decisions so that the next person, or their AI, has enough context to avoid making the same mistakes. Cohesion requires context.

It seems we’ve accelerated our ability to make things, but not necessarily our ability to understand their implications.

## Enjoy it sustainably[Direct link](#enjoy-sustainably "Link to this section")

I don’t want to go back or slow down. In fact, I want **more** of this. I just want to do it sustainably. We can’t keep accelerating what an organisation can produce while expecting a handful of people to hold everything together.

And before you say it, throwing more agents at the problem isn’t necessarily going to help.

I think the opportunity lies in distributing judgement. Shared principles, design systems that communicate intent, and better ways to preserve decisions and their context. We need to scale our ability to understand how things **fit together**, not just our ability to build them.

Frustratingly, I’m not convinced anyone has figured out how to do this at the speed we’re now moving. Certainly not me. And until we do, I suspect the people who care most about keeping things coherent will continue absorbing the cost.

Coherence isn’t about protecting the way things have always been done. It’s about making sure that as our products evolve, they continue to make sense as a whole. That should be a collective responsibility, not something maintained through constant attention.

Because good, fast and cheap aren’t much of a victory if we’re still paying for them with our own energy. Maybe the old diagram needs updating: _Good, cheap, fast and coherent_. Pick three.
