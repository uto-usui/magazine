We're in a moment where it has never been cheaper or faster to build something convincing. The cost of taking an idea and making it look real, feel functional, or seem finished has collapsed. That is genuinely good news if you already know what you're building and why. It's dangerous if you don't.

I believe the bottleneck right now is intentionality. Not execution. We have execution in abundance. What we're short on is the clarity that makes execution worth anything.

Here are four clarifications I’d like to make at this moment.

## 1\. Design is a process, not an artifact

Design gets talked about like it's a deliverable. Screens, mocks, prototypes, guidelines — those are artifacts of design, but they aren't design. Design is the act of deciding what should exist, why it should exist, and what "right" looks like before you've committed to building it.

That's true for a UI. But it's also true for a GTM plan, a brand video, an API architecture, a sales narrative, or a pricing strategy. The artifacts change, but the underlying act is the same: someone has to define the expectation before anyone can execute against it. Someone has to know what good looks like before it exists.

That distinction matters a lot right now because AI cannot do that part. You intend to do something that has not yet happened. You have to bring those parameters to the table to do anything novel. AI doesn't know your constraints. It doesn't know your strategy. It doesn't know what moment in the market you're in, what your team is trying to prove, or what your customers actually need versus what they've said they want. The expectation — the definition of what good looks like — is something only you can provide. AI's job is to meet that expectation. Not to define it.

If you hand that job off, you're not saving time. You're just skipping the most important step. That important step is what I want to be synonymous with the word "Figma."

## 2\. AI accelerates execution, not clarity

Throwing AI at something isn't free, especially if it's fast. Compute costs real money. Time costs real money. Attention costs real money. You can generate an artifact in minutes, but it might take hours to actually review and correct. Something subtly wrong at step one tends to become a structural problem by step five...but I want to talk about another cost.

The most expensive thing you can do isn't build something badly. It's to execute well on the wrong idea. Rolling back a feature has a huge cost. That's how businesses go under — not bad code, not slow teams, but teams that were genuinely good building things no one needed. AI doesn't protect you from that failure mode. It actually accelerates it.

Here's what this confusion can look like today. Someone has an idea. They use AI to build a demo. The output is polished. It feels considered. It looks finished. And then someone in the room says "why should we do this?" and the answer is just a replay of the demo's features instead of a core problem statement and description of the solution.

That demo is not an argument. It's a shiny object. A polished artifact alone does not inherently answer "why does this exist?" or "does anyone need this?" Making something look more real doesn't make the underlying case any stronger. It often makes it harder for people in the room to push back. Polish is not a substitute for a case. If anything, it makes it harder to demand one.

## 3\. 100 times zero is zero

The fix isn't to use AI less. It's to use AI in the right order, and to apply it to something worth multiplying. Invest in the idea first. Work out what it is, why it matters, what good looks like — you can use AI to help. Only after that process is when you should use AI to realize it.

A concrete example: writing skills for AI. If you ask AI to write a skill from scratch you've handed off the most important part of the job. A skill isn't a configuration detail. It encodes an expectation: here is what good looks like for this specific thing, in this specific context. Deciding what that is is design work. It requires knowing what outcome you're after and being able to evaluate whether you got there with that skill.

If you start with a well-defined objective and collection of source context, AI can help you improve a skill you've already written. It can refine and stress-test it. That works because you brought the intentionality. The expectation already exists and AI is filling in toward a target.

You define the targets. Then use AI to help you reach them. Reverse that and you're optimizing toward nothing — you're putting a lot of effort and sinking a lot of AI cost into a shallow output you haven't actually considered or defined.

## 4\. Collaboration amplifies intent

Intentionality earlier in the process makes execution better downstream. When work is shared, that effect multiplies — shared clarity before execution means less correction after. That's not just a product story. It's how design has always worked. I've seen it play out for years. And it's more true now than it has ever been.

It applies to how any of us work together on anything. Every feature you ship, every campaign you launch, every skill you write, every demo you put in front of a customer, every internal narrative you try to build: the same logic applies. _What is this? Why does it exist? What does good look like before we start executing?_

That's not a design team question. It's not a PM question. It's the question anyone doing intentional work should be asking, regardless of what they're building. The discipline is the same whether you're shipping a UI or a positioning doc or a data pipeline or planning an event.

Thoughtfulness is not a bottleneck. It's the investment that makes everything else worth doing. AI is a multiplier on human intention, not a substitute for it. Believing that isn't a messaging position — it's a practice.

* * *

It may surprise you to learn that I used AI to write this. The structure, the sentences, a lot of the phrasing — _generated_. But the argument existed before any of it. I knew what I was trying to say. I knew what examples mattered and which ones were wrong. I knew when a paragraph was close but not quite right, and I revised toward a target I'd already defined. Now, I love using these tools, I use them all the time. But to write this, I was the one doing the design work, even when AI was doing the writing. That's the point.

The tools changed. The work didn't. Design is the process. Design is the intentionality. Design is the expectation you set before you execute. And design isn't a role — it's a practice. Anyone considering a path to an outcome is doing design, whether they call it that or not. The engineer deciding how a system should behave. The marketer deciding what story to tell and why. The team deciding what to build next.

There is nothing more costly than shipping something that shouldn't exist. And the fact that execution is now faster and cheaper doesn't change that. If anything, it makes clarity at the front of the process more important than it's ever been.
