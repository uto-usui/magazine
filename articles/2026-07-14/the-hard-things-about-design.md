I once wrote in [16 pieces of design wisdom](https://hvpandya.com/design-wisdom) that design requires a tremendous amount of mental fortitude. One line in a list of sixteen, for something that deserves an essay of its own.

This one is for the folks deep into the vocation. The veterans running teams, the senior craftspeople who’ve been at this for a decade or more. We know the romance of the craft. What we don’t talk about as much is its costs, the ones we keep absorbing every day, and why design carries costs that engineering and product management mostly avoid. So here’s an accounting of what it costs us, and why.

**Inspiration for design can come from anywhere.** A design solution comes from a pattern match, and that’s what makes the search space so wide. An engineer chasing a caching bug searches a bounded space: known data structures, known algorithms, code that exists in a library or in last year’s sprint. A product manager sizing a new market searches a bounded space too: comparable companies, growth curves, unit economics that follow shapes we’ve all seen before. We don’t get a bounded space. The answer to a checkout flow might be sitting in how a hotel runs its check-in line, or how a gate agent boards a flight, or a wayfinding system in a museum we walked through years ago. Carrying around a private library of everything we’ve ever paid attention to, and pulling from it mid conversation with no warning that this is the moment we’ll need it, is just how the job works day to day. I once solved an onboarding screen with a wayfinding detail I remembered from a museum visit years ago, and I wasn’t looking for it when it surfaced. An engineer stuck on a problem can look it up: documentation, a colleague, a forum post from someone who solved the same thing last year. We’re searching our own memory of lived experience instead, and if we haven’t lived it, noticed it, or filed it away somewhere, it isn’t available to us no matter how long we search. That’s the part that makes it uniquely hard: the spark either lives in us, or we don’t have one to begin with.

**The path to breakthroughs is non-linear, and it doesn’t repeat.** Most crafts compound with repetition: an engineer’s tenth caching fix reuses patterns validated in the ninth, a product manager’s tenth pricing model reuses assumptions tested in the ninth. Our tenth breakthrough tends to cost about the same as the first one did, because the route that got us to our last great piece of work rarely gets us to the next one. Some weeks the answer comes out of a rough sketch on day two. Other weeks it takes forty odd variants and a hallway conversation no one planned, and there’s no telling in advance which kind of week we’re about to have, not for us and not for anyone reviewing the work. This is exactly where design diverges from engineering: an engineer who has solved fifty similar bugs recognizes the fifty first one faster, because the underlying pattern repeats. A designer who has cracked fifty onboarding flows doesn’t crack the fifty first one faster, because each one draws on a different combination of references, and there’s no accumulated shortcut for combination itself. Experience makes us better searchers. It doesn’t hand us the answer.

**Design is legible to everyone.** A codebase requires literacy to critique. A go to market strategy requires business literacy to critique. A screen requires nothing: anyone who has ever used a phone has an opinion the instant they see one, and that opinion arrives whether we asked for it or not. Feedback shows up constantly and from everywhere, in Slack threads, in hallway comments, in a founder’s aside during an unrelated meeting, ranging from a passing complaint about a color to precise, well reasoned craft critique, and all of it has to be triaged without dismissing the naive version, because the naive reaction is often exactly what a real user will have too. An engineer’s code review comes from other engineers who share vocabulary. A product manager’s strategy document gets reviewed by people trained to reason about markets. Our reviewers are everyone in the building and plenty of people outside it, and none of them needed training to have an opinion. Design draws more scrutiny than other crafts for the simple reason that it’s the only kind of work almost anyone can react to on sight.

**The mental cost of interactions is the price we pay for high quality outcomes.** A novelist reaches their best draft alone at a desk over months of quiet. An engineer reaches their best solution through their own iteration, running it, profiling it, fixing it, with a code review at the end that’s a single pass. We reach ours through repeated exposure to the same decision, explained again to people who weren’t in the room for the last explanation. In a single week that can mean presenting the same flow five separate times: the crit, the one on one with the product manager, the stakeholder readout, the leadership review, the user testing session, and re-litigating the same decision from scratch each time. Every one of those conversations makes the work better. Every one of them also takes something out of us that the work itself still needed for the next round. A product manager defends a decision to a narrower, more senior room. We defend the same decision to a wider room, over and over, and the reasoning has to be rebuilt each time because visual work doesn’t come with the shorthand a metrics dashboard gives a product manager.

**Obsession becomes an important tool, and it drains the mind.** An engineer has linters, type systems, and test suites that catch a large share of mistakes before a person ever looks. A product manager has data: an experiment moves the metric or it doesn’t, and that closes the question either way. We don’t have an equivalent instrument. The only tool that can tell us whether a corner radius sits wrong, or a transition lands a beat too slow, is a trained eye doing another pass, and then another. That’s why we reopen a file at 11pm over a spacing issue no one had flagged, or rebuild an entire flow the night before a review because the seams bothered us and no one else had noticed them yet. Real satisfaction with our work is rare, and it’s the result of exactly this kind of obsession, because every additional pass peels back something the previous one had hidden. What makes it hard in a way engineering and product work aren’t is that this polish pass can’t be delegated to a tool. It has to be done by a person, over and over, and running our own eyes over the same screen for the sixth time is tiring in a way that reading a test result or a dashboard isn’t.

**We have to become comfortable with permanent dissatisfaction with how things are.** A bridge holds a load and the job is done. A print run ships and the book is finished. An engineer’s bug fix is closed once the test passes, and the test is the proof. A product manager’s feature either moves the metric or it doesn’t, and the data renders a verdict either way, closing the question even when the answer is failure. Design’s version of done doesn’t get a scoreboard like that. The moment something goes live, we see how people use it, and that observation becomes the next problem to solve. A launch feels good for about an afternoon, and by evening we’re listing what v2 needs to fix, because the standard we hold ourselves to moved the instant we saw the thing out in the world. What makes this uniquely hard is that the instrument measuring done is our own taste, and taste recalibrates the moment we clear the last bar, so there’s no external, objective signal that ever tells us to stop. The cruel irony is that getting better at this only raises the bar we hold ourselves to, so satisfaction doesn’t catch up.

**Creativity does not respect a calendar.** A design review depends on an idea turning up, and unlike a status update or an estimate, an idea can’t be manufactured on demand by running a known process. Inspiration doesn’t take bookings for a Tuesday 4pm review. The review happens at 4pm either way. Some weeks the thinking clicks on Monday morning in the shower. Other weeks it hasn’t shown up by Tuesday lunch, and we walk in with whatever we’ve got. An engineer without a clever solution can still ship the straightforward version, because correctness doesn’t require elegance. A product manager without a bold strategy can still produce a competent prioritization matrix from the data on hand, because the method works even without a spark. Design rarely has an equivalent fallback that produces something good on its own. The senior version of this job is to walk in with the idea anyway, and that reliability took years of reps to build, one rep at a time, so that on the days nothing comes, craft is what covers for the missing magic.

**For original work, understanding art becomes as important as the mechanics of experience.** Patterns and heuristics get us to competence, and that’s as far as they take us. The difference shows up between a screen that follows every rule correctly and still feels forgettable, and a screen that breaks a rule on purpose and feels inevitable, and knowing which rule to break, and when, is something we pick up from time spent paying attention to things that have nothing to do with software: why a Kubrick frame feels inevitable, or why a Braun radio from the 60s looks right after sixty years. Engineering and product work both have a syllabus for advancing past competence, a systems design course, a growth framework, a case study we can sit down and work through. Art has no syllabus. That same kind of knowledge, the kind that lets us choose an original decision over a correct one, comes from decades of paying attention to things outside our tools, and no curriculum compresses that timeline.

**It takes years of prolificity to fill the gap between our aspiration and our craft.** Taste, the ability to recognize good work, develops faster than skill, the ability to produce it, and design is one of the few crafts where we feel that gap every day rather than have it caught for us. Our taste runs ahead of our skill. That gap is what got us into this in the first place, and it’s also what tells us, every day, that our work isn’t good enough yet. Ira Glass called this the taste gap. His answer was simple: make more work. A lot more. Most of it will be bad. He meant for it to be that way, because volume is the only thing that closes the gap. In engineering, correctness is checkable: a compiler catches a broken function no matter how much taste the engineer has, so a junior’s gap between aspiration and skill rarely makes it into production. In design, competent but tasteless work still ships, still does the job in the functional sense, and nothing stops it except the discomfort of noticing it ourselves. We have to care enough to keep going after most attempts fail. There’s no shortcut. We close the gap one piece of work at a time, and that takes years.

**Obsession is a lonely pursuit.** A broken build blocks a merge for everyone, so quality in engineering is partly enforced by the system itself, through tests and continuous integration that block a bad merge no matter who wrote it. A weak strategy tends to get caught in review by people trained to catch it. Design rarely has an equivalent gate. Nothing stops a mediocre screen from shipping the way a failing test stops a broken function, so the only thing holding the line is whoever cares enough to hold it, and that’s most often one person, alone. Midnight finds us each alone with a corner radius while the project channel is off discussing launch dates, and no one else in the building is paid to care about that decision at the depth we care about it. Our colleagues have their own fires to fight, our timelines have no room for our standards, and the negotiation we run the most often is with our own well-being. It’s a structural gap, no one is coming to enforce the bar for us, so either we hold it or it doesn’t get held.

**A thousand little details wear us down.** A trained eye doesn’t come with an off switch. It rewires how we see, the same way we can’t look at a sentence and not read it, and once that circuit is built, it runs on everything we look at, the product we’re paid to work on and everything else we happen to be looking at that day. The misaligned padding and the label that runs one word too long follow us home. We’ve sat at dinner on a Saturday night and caught ourselves judging the kerning on the menu. An engineer’s trained eye for a code smell only fires when they’re looking at code, a bounded, occupation specific surface, and no one reviews the syntax of a restaurant menu on their night off. Our training generalizes to every visual and spatial surface we encounter, because alignment, spacing, and hierarchy apply to everything we see, not just our screens, and the perception we built for work doesn’t clock out.

**The only way to refine our work is to go through years of prolificity.** An engineer’s solved problem becomes a reusable library, a documented pattern that saves time on the next project at a different company. A product manager’s playbook, a pricing model or an onboarding funnel that worked, travels well to a new product. Our taste doesn’t travel the same way, because what looks right for one brand or product often doesn’t for another, so the calibration has to be rebuilt through volume nearly every time. Our cutting room floor stays full of a million iterations across every new project, and that sheer volume is what refines our internal sense of quality enough to produce it on command in whatever context we land in next, more than any formula ever could. There’s no studying our way into this one. The discarded work itself is what trains us, and it has to happen again for every new context, because the taste we built for the last one doesn’t carry over.

If you’re leading designers, watch for this. Your best people are carrying all of it while making the work look effortless, so protect them. And if that’s you carrying it, the exhaustion means you’re doing it right.

We’d pick this craft again without any hesitation. Engineering and product work don’t ask this much of anyone. We’d pick it anyway. Now, back to work.
