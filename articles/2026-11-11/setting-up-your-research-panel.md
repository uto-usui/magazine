_This is part three of a_ [_four part series_](https://medium.com/@thomas.n.anne/the-realities-of-participant-panels-c41463fc9a14?sharedUserId=thomas.n.anne) _on panel management._

A participant panel is easy to picture as a thing: a tool, a list, or a group of people who answer your surveys. It’s more useful to picture it as a lifecycle. Where you are in that lifecycle determines what you should be working on right now, and understanding that progression is the fastest way to see how the pieces of this series fit together.

We think of setup in four phases:

-   **Decide.** Do you need a panel at all, and does it make more sense to build one or buy access? (That’s the ground we covered in [the build vs buy piece.)](https://medium.com/@thomas.n.anne/the-realities-of-participant-panels-c41463fc9a14?sharedUserId=thomas.n.anne)
-   **Advocate.** Make the case. Secure the mandate, the budget, and leadership support needed to launch a panel. (Covered in [the advocacy piece](https://medium.com/@thomas.n.anne/participant-panels-why-research-teams-build-them-and-when-they-should-buy-instead-157b1b8e4060?sharedUserId=thomas.n.anne).)
-   **Set up.** Turn that approval into a working panel: the right stakeholders at the table, the right tooling for your scale, clear recruitment criteria, and your first participants.
-   **Sustain.** Keep the panel healthy so it continues delivering value year after year instead of quietly fading away. (More on that in [the panel management piece](https://medium.com/@thomas.n.anne/managing-and-maintaining-a-healthy-research-panel-cc63af0b2a1f?sharedUserId=thomas.n.anne).)

This article focuses on the third phase. It picks up where the previous article leaves off: you have approval, resources, and a mandate. Now it’s time to set up the panel you were funded to create.

Stakeholders appear in both phases, which can make it seem like we’re covering the same ground twice. We’re not. Advocacy is about persuasion: convincing leadership that a panel is worth funding and supporting. Setup is about execution: coordinating with legal on consent and governance, with sales and customer success to protect client relationships, and with marketing to ensure your first recruitment campaign doesn’t collide with a major launch or outreach effort.

No, advocacy doesn’t end the moment the budget clears. You’ll keep re-earning buy-in long after. But you have to start building somewhere. This is where.

## Who to Involve Before You Build Anything

Setting up a participant panel is not a solo project. The practitioners who have done it well are consistent on this point: the biggest mistakes happen when someone tries to build in isolation and ends up asking for favors rather than collaborating on a shared resource.

Getting the right people involved early (and being clear about what you need from each of them) is as much a part of setup as choosing your tool or writing your first recruitment email.

A RACI framework maps this cleanly.

![RACI table for setting up a research panel. Responsible: ReOps lead or researchers, owning tooling, recruitment, communications, and tracking. Accountable: Director of Research or Head of Insights, owning budget, mandate, and sign-off. Consulted: Legal, IT, Marketing, Sales, Finance. Informed: PMs, designers, support, and account management.](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*dLN1_c9RknkwNxWtKxv2pQ.png)

-   **Research Operations & Researchers —** In mature organizations, a ResearchOps lead typically owns the infrastructure build: tool selection, recruitment workflows, incentive administration, and communication templates. In smaller teams, a researcher may carry those responsibilities alone. Either way, someone must be explicitly accountable before a single recruitment email goes out.
-   **Content Design —** If your organization includes content designers, involve them early. The voice and tone of recruitment emails, panel landing pages, consent language, and participant communications shape expectations from the very first interaction.
-   **Management —** Someone with organizational authority needs to champion the effort. Without executive support, budget approvals become “pulling teeth” (as one researcher described her own experience) and every cross-functional request turns into a negotiation. Secure buy-in before you need it.
-   **Legal & Compliance** — Legal and compliance should be involved early, not because they slow the process down, but because the guardrails they establish shape everything from consent and data retention to participant communications and Personally Identifiable Information (PII) handling. As one researcher explained: “Those initial conversations with the legal team to establish formalized terms and conditions really helped us set up guardrails. It was helpful to know what _not_ to do in order to know what we should do starting out.”
-   **Marketing and CRM —** Marketing is easy to overlook until you’ve scheduled a recruitment campaign that conflicts with a product launch or customer outreach initiative. Partner early to coordinate communications, avoid customer fatigue, and gain access to existing CRM expertise and channels.
-   **Product, Design, & Customer Support —** These teams don’t necessarily need to help build the panel, but they do need to understand what it is, why it exists, and how to use it. Customer support teams, in particular, are often the first to hear from participants checking whether a recruitment email is legitimate. A simple internal briefing before launch can prevent confusion and build credibility from day one.
-   **Sales & Customer Success —** In B2B environments, Sales and Customer Success own the customer relationship and can either facilitate access or effectively block it. Involve them early so they understand how participants will be approached, what safeguards are in place, and how the panel benefits both customers and the business.

**Case Study: Orrin Ward, Design and ReOps Lead in the FinTech space.** Orrin’s team was in the process of launching their panel when they realized they underestimated how protective their sales team would be of customer access. As a result, researchers found themselves repeatedly blocked from contacting users directly.

“We pivoted halfway through and realized we needed to build a better relationship with commercial, build trust with them so that we don’t have to keep fighting to be able to talk to our users.” The team restructured its approach to include commercial (Sales, Customer Success) as a partner in customer communications about the panel, not a gatekeeper to route around. Response rates improved. So did the internal reputation of the research function.

## Choosing Your Tooling: Spreadsheet, Existing CRM, or Dedicated Platform?

The tooling question is a balancing act. Invest too early and you risk buying capabilities you don’t yet need. Invest too late and you’ll spend valuable time managing logistics manually that even basic tooling could have automated. Your organizational context will also impact the choice a lot — for instance, is there more time or money easily available? Is it easy or hard to get new vendors through procurement? Does your leadership feel that having their own toolset is important to signal differentiation and importance internally? If you end up with a long running panel, you’ll experience plenty of change, so you will be well served to monitor when conditions have changed enough to make the case to change what kind of system support is in place.

### **The home-grown method**

Many of the professionals we talked to have used a spreadsheet at some point while creating their panels. And for some specialist populations, spreadsheets are sufficient altogether. One ReOps Lead, for example, manages a panel of 200–300 clinical scientists on a spreadsheet. At that scale, with that population, a dedicated tool would add overhead without adding value. The spreadsheet _is_ the panel.

### **Spreadsheet as Proof of Concept**

The scrappy tool that gets you going can be the prototype that leads to better things. The most common method we heard from practitioners was the spreadsheet-as-proof-of-concept approach. Starting “lean” allowed practitioners to demonstrate the utility of an always-on panel before advocating for tooling investment.

The people we spoke with said there is generally a natural tipping point: once you’re managing more than a few hundred contacts, tracking who’s been contacted, who’s been paid, and who’s been over-used becomes a job in itself.

**Case Study: Jen Cardey, Research Operations Manager, Travel & Hospitality Platform.**

Jen made the switch at around 2,500 contacts, after managing her panel in a spreadsheet for years. She inherited a panel maintained in a Google Sheet with 2,500 manually tracked contacts, and it was becoming unwieldy. The decision to migrate wasn’t driven by ambition; it was driven by pain. Tracking participation, managing incentives, and maintaining data hygiene had become unsustainable.

She moved to a panel management platform, used it to build a second B2C panel alongside the original, and then launched a self-service research program, “Research Lite,” that opened panel access to engineers, designers, and product managers across the organization.

The shift didn’t just solve the logistics problem. It changed what the panel was capable of. “We can get research turnaround in a couple of weeks. It’s so much faster than we ever expected.”

Based on our interviews, here are some other common signs that it’s time to invest in dedicated panel management tooling:

-   **You’re over-utilizing the same people.** If you have no automated way to track participation frequency, you may find that your most responsive panelists start to be overrepresented in your studies.
-   **You’re burning out the participants who do respond**. Too many invitations can lead to survey fatigue, causing even engaged participants to disengage or leave the panel entirely.
-   **Compliance risk is too high.** Managing opt-outs, consent records, or PII in a shared spreadsheet might be too risky for your industry or location. As Senior Research Operations Specialist Emily Wever framed it: “Spending a little money on a proper tool now saves a ton compared to regulatory scrutiny later.”
-   **Recruitment is blocking research.** If logistics like scheduling, incentives, and reminders are taking more time than the actual research, automation is no longer a luxury. It’s the path back to doing the work.

### **Using the Existing CRM**

Not every organization jumps directly from spreadsheets to a dedicated panel management platform. For teams that already have a customer database or CRM in place, the next logical step may be to leverage that existing infrastructure before investing in a specialized solution.

In many organizations, the CRM already contains customer profiles, contact histories, segmentation data, and communication workflows. While it wasn’t designed specifically for research operations, it can often support the early stages of panel management and provide a bridge between a home-grown spreadsheet and a dedicated panel platform.

Advantages

-   **Less duplication of PII.** Customer data already stored in the CRM may reduce the need to create and govern a separate participant database.
-   **No new vendor.** Vendor selection, procurement, and onboarding can be lengthy processes. The CRM is already in place.
-   **Leverages existing investments.** Instead of introducing another tool, you’re getting more value from technology the organization already owns and supports.

Disadvantages

-   **You’re a small fish in a big pond.** It can be difficult to get the features, support, or changes you need when the system is primarily optimized for other, larger, and better-funded parts of the organization.
-   **Keeping a bright line between research and marketing.** For anti-spam compliance, participant trust, and expectation management, it’s often helpful to have a clear separation between marketing communications and research activities.
-   **Systems can be complex.** CRMs are powerful, but they weren’t necessarily designed for research operations. Without dedicated support or customization, they can be difficult to learn, navigate, and adapt to your needs.

### **Specialized Panel Management Systems**

For other teams, the risks and downsides to the spreadsheet convinced them to invest in tooling early. To understand if your context calls for specialized tooling, evaluate these tooling upsides.

-   **Better coordination with your Sales or Marketing CRM.** Spreadsheets don’t easily talk to your CRM, which means you might inadvertently send a survey invite to a customer who is in the middle of a contract renewal.
-   **Tooling can enforce a cooldown period.** With manual spreadsheet management, it’s common for enthusiastic participants to end up dominating your participant pool. Most panel management systems have cooldown periods designed to prevent researchers from over-engaging participants.
-   **Panel management systems are designed with compliance in mind.** Right-to-be-forgotten requests and PII protection are often problems that stay invisible until legal gets involved. Dedicated platforms may provide features that make consent, deletion requests, and PII management easier to administer consistently.

Whether you choose a spreadsheet, an existing CRM, or a dedicated panel management platform, the technology is ultimately a supporting decision. The bigger determinant of success is whether your panel contains the participants you’ll need six months from now, not just the participants who are easiest to recruit today.

Before recruiting your first participant, you need to define what success looks like and who the panel needs to represent.

## Build Your Panel Success Criteria

Before the first recruitment email goes out, you need a clear answer to a question most teams skip: who, exactly, is this panel for? Teams that skip this step build panels that work for the first few studies and quietly fail when a stakeholder needs a segment that was never recruited for.

**Start with your research agenda**

The most common mistake in panel planning is starting with whoever is easiest to reach and working backward to justify the composition. Consider a 12-month snapshot of the research you expect to run. Sketch enough of a picture to ask: what segments will we need to hear from, and how often? That question surfaces your panel’s size and segment requirements faster than any tool selection or recruitment channel decision will.

A team running monthly pulse surveys needs a panel large enough to sustain repeated outreach without burning participants out. A team doing longitudinal qualitative work may only need eight to ten deeply engaged people.

Joanne Van Vranken, Research Operations Manager in the accounting software industry, had a B2B panel that peaked at 31 members and was ultimately judged a failure, not because 31 is inherently too small, but because the organization needed statistical analysis and the panel couldn’t support it. The failure wasn’t in the recruitment. It was in the mismatch between what the panel was built for and what it was eventually asked to do.

### **Define Your Segments**

One of the beauties of having your own panel is being able to talk to specific sub-sets of users based on your research needs. To do that, the panel needs to contain relevant data to understand who is available. But keeping the initial sign-up form short means being selective about what you ask upfront (versus what you collect through profiling surveys over time).

Our experts were opinionated on what to capture on day one to save yourself from future headaches.

-   **Role, seniority, company size, industry, and product tier or subscription level** are typically non-negotiables in B2B contexts. As Tanya Branitska, a market researcher in the HR software space who built a developer panel, put it: “The number one question was, what’s their subscription tier? What do they do? Because the research often is dependent on that section of the business.” Agustina Di Clemente, a Research Operations Specialist in B2B travel & Spend Management Software, took a similarly deliberate approach, aligning her segments directly with quarterly research priorities, including market size, company size, and user behavior. She reviewed and updated those segments each quarter as priorities shifted.
-   **Geography and demographics** emerged as consistently relevant for government contexts. Arun Upadhyay, Research Ops Specialist in the market research industry, built quotas into his panel from launch, matching census data for age and gender so results could be trusted to reflect the actual population.
-   **Tenure and product use** proved particularly relevant for B2C contexts where power users often need to be studied separately from recently onboarded users.
-   **Participation metadata** is valuable from the start, regardless of context: date of last contact, study participation history, and notes about past participation and contributions. Teams that skip this risk re-screening people they’ve already spoken with or repeatedly drawing on their most enthusiastic respondents without realizing it.

## **What Practitioners Wish They’d Tracked Earlier**

The most consistent regret across practitioners was what they hadn’t captured about participants early on.

-   **Recruitment source.** Karen Bills, a Research Ops Specialist across marketplaces and social media platforms, wished she’d tagged every participant with their original recruitment source. As her program grew and satellite communities merged into the main panel, she lost the ability to cleanly distinguish “gen pop” from “premium” participants because the sourcing data wasn’t there. “Design for the next ask, not just the immediate need,” she said. “That was a learning pain we felt over the years.”
-   **Incentive preference**. Arya Guinney, a Consumer Insights Researcher at a national restaurant chain, built an incentive preference question into her screener from the start and called it one of her best early decisions. Knowing whether participants wanted branded gift cards or neutral rewards like Amazon helped her make better use of the incentive budget while supporting response rates.
-   **Behavioral personas over job titles.** Job titles can be unreliable proxies for actual behavior. “IT Admin” and “Marketer” can mean different things at different companies. Teams that captured capabilities and tasks upfront, what people actually do day to day, found those attributes more durable for targeting than job titles.
-   **Verification signals.** With AI-generated survey responses creating new quality concerns, several practitioners said they wished they’d collected LinkedIn URLs or other verification markers at sign-up. Verifying participants and identifying questionable responses becomes much harder retroactively than at the point of recruitment.
-   **Account health in B2B.** Integrating CRM data early, or at minimum flagging participants by relationship status, can help prevent an uncomfortable situation like sending a research invitation to a customer who is in a tense contract renewal. It’s the kind of detail that may not feel urgent until it suddenly is.

**How big does my panel need to be?**

We are often asked how large a panel needs to be to become successful, or even “worth it.” What we learned from our practitioner interviews is that panel size is better treated as a calculation than a target.

A basic starting point is:

_\[(Desired responses per study / Expected response rate) x Number of studies per month\]_ +10–20% buffer for attrition and non-response

For example, if you need 20 completed responses per study, expect a 40% response rate, and plan to run four studies per month, you’d need 50 participants per study, or 200 across the month. Add a 15% buffer for attrition and non-response, and your estimated panel size is about 230 participants.

_This is a starting estimate. Your actual needs will vary based many factors such as on how often participants can take part, how narrowly studies are targeted, and how much participant overlap exists between studies. Modelling or estimating panel size can get as elaborate as you want, or beyond._

But size isn’t everything. A few practical principles matter just as much as the math:

-   **Engagement beats scale.** “It doesn’t tell me any story if I just tell you that I have a panel of 10k users if they’re not engaged. If I have a 1k panel with very engaged people, that will give you better performance,” says Agustina Di Clemente, a Research Operations Specialist in B2B Travel & Spend Management Software.
-   **Manageability sets a real ceiling.** A panel is only as useful as your ability to manage it. The right size is one your team can recruit, maintain, segment, communicate with, and learn from consistently.

Keep all data and processing local to the browser.

## Start Recruiting

Once your team is bought in, your tooling selected, and the recruitment criteria identified, it’s time to start recruiting your panelists. The advice from practitioners was consistent: cold outreach to unfamiliar audiences should be a last resort, not a starting point. Begin with the warm channels already available to you.

**Start with warm channels**

-   **Internal CRM databases** were consistently cited as strong sources for the first wave of outreach. Jen Cardey, a Research Operations Manager, tapped her existing customer database and reached 7,000 sign-ups within one to two months. The advantage isn’t just reach, but relevance: people who already have a relationship with the brand may be more receptive to joining and participating.
-   **Past research participants** can also be a fruitful resource. Agustina found that re-engaging people who had participated in research over the previous three years was “one of the initiatives that performed really well.” These are people who have already said yes to research once.
-   **Marketing and product touchpoints** are worth including in the first wave as well. In-product recruitment, customer communications, and email updates can put the invitation in front of people who already have some connection to the product or brand.

Across practitioner accounts, these warm channels offered compelling examples of both speed and quality. One team using a two-channel approach of Slack and email received 70% of its responses within an hour. Others found that people recruited through warm channels provided richer feedback, in part because they were invested in improving tools they already used.

**Avoid generic lists**

One consistent trap: an existing list isn’t necessarily the right list. Avinash Mangar discovered this while managing a WWE panel, where merchandise purchaser lists included parents and relatives buying gifts, not necessarily the engaged fans the research needed to reach. “Reaching out through our engaged CRM base was much better.” The lesson: availability is no substitute for relevance.

**Consider niche communities**

When standard channels fall short, consider where specialized populations already gather and how their qualifications can be verified.

**Case Study: Douglas Wicks, Senior Research Manager and Consultant across Health, Telecomm, and Financial** Douglas needed licensed hairstylists for a study, a population that consumer panels couldn’t reliably deliver. Initial panel responses were “bogus” and required extensive sifting before useful data emerged. His solution was to purchase a specialized trade magazine subscriber list, then validate participants against state licensing records. The list was expensive. The validation was manual. But it gave him access to the qualified population he needed, and the resulting panel became a resource for future studies with the same audience.

### **Reach skeptical audiences in person**

For audiences that may be skeptical of research, such as fraud investigators, clinical specialists, and regulated professionals, personal credibility can matter more than another recruitment email. Jess Ivins, a Senior UX Researcher in the enterprise software space, found that cold emails to customers produced “crickets.” What worked was going on-site, meeting people face to face, and letting them see who was asking and why.

> “I realized I have to go on-site and build face-to-face relationships with these customers so they know who I am.”

### **Get creative to reach harder-to-find audiences**

For hard-to-reach demographics, traditional recruitment channels may be tough. Practitioners described getting creative about both where they recruited and how they removed barriers to participation. Joe Jordan, President of Elliam, Inc., described one company that built a panel of teenagers ages 14 to 17 whose parents had already provided standing consent, making it easier to recruit from that population for future studies.

The through-line across these examples is simple: recruit with the audience in mind. That might mean working through an account manager, recruiting in person at a conference booth, partnering with a community organization, or relying on peer referrals. For hard-to-reach populations, the best recruitment path may look very different than the broad outreach that works elsewhere.

Building a panel is relatively easy. Building one that remains useful and engaged over time is harder. There are too many contextual factors for any single formula or process to guarantee success. But the practitioners we spoke with pointed to a consistent set of principles: work iteratively, find your allies and sponsors, address objections early, and understand what your organization needs today while planning for what it needs tomorrow. Those practices give your panel a better foundation for becoming a durable research resource rather than a short-lived initiative.

## What to Do After Setup

After considering the guidance above, you may have everything in place to launch your panel. Contacting that first wave of participants is a major milestone, but don’t mistake it for the finish line.

The failure mode for panels is rarely a bad launch. It’s the quiet fade: the panel that opens with fanfare, runs well for a quarter or two, then slowly goes silent as participants churn, profiles go stale, and the people who championed it move on to the next thing. A panel isn’t a project you finish. It’s a resource you keep alive.
