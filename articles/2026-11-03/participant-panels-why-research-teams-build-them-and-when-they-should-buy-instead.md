If you’ve spent any meaningful time doing research, you already know the feeling: a project lands, the brief is clear, and then you spend weeks in preparation alone. Finding people. Chasing responses. Coordinating calendars. Sending the same email for the fifth time.

Recruitment is a persistent operational bottleneck in research. “The biggest time sink in UX research is finding, recruiting, and identifying participants,” [as one VP of Digital Products put it.](https://www.alida.com/the-alida-journal/how-to-recruit-participants-for-ux-research)

Teams that have built participant panels describe a different experience entirely: one where the infrastructure is established before research needs emerge. But before building a panel, consider these factors when deciding how to proceed.

### Benefits of an Owned Panel

#### Speed and Efficiency

The numbers are hard to argue with. According to [User Interviews](https://www.userinterviews.com/ux-research-roi-cost-savings-report-2023), to source 50 B2B participants, ad-hoc recruitment can take ~47x more labor hours than when done through owned panels (165 labor hours vs. 3.5). Across the industry, community-based recruitment like those that source from owned panels is cited as [5X faster](https://www.alida.com/the-alida-journal/how-to-recruit-participants-for-ux-research) than ad hoc methods, with some teams receiving answers to UX questions within a single day.

The goal most practitioners describe is getting in front of users within a standard 1–2 week sprint, something that’s nearly impossible when you’re sourcing from scratch. One Customer Insights Analyst in the utilities space described the appeal to stakeholders plainly:

> “We could put in a few minute survey on a Friday and then on Monday morning, we have all these results and we can analyze it. It’s super fast where everything else would take a lot longer.”

### Self-Service and Democratization

One of the less-discussed benefits of a functioning panel is what it unlocks for people who aren’t researchers. Paired with other ReOp infrastructure like guardrails, templates, and guides, panels allow product managers and engineers to run their own studies without sitting on a research roadmap or waiting for UXR capacity.

As Rebecca Okoroji, UX Researcher in IT services, put it:

> “There’s no way we can run the studies for all of \[our stakeholders\]. So what we try to do is enable them to talk to customers or users themselves. The panel was meant to be broad, available, and people can self-serve.”

**Case Study: Jen Cardey, Research Operations Manager, Travel Marketplaces** Jen inherited a panel living on a Google Sheet, containing roughly 2,500 contacts, manually managed, and hard to scale. She migrated it into a panel management tool and then launched “Research Lite,” a self-service program that opened panel access to engineers, designers, and PMs across the product org. For Jen’s team, the reaction from stakeholders was immediate: “We can just come to you, get a research turnaround in a couple of weeks. It’s so much faster than we ever expected.”

### Quality, Depth, and Longitudinal Potential

Panels can improve more than speed. Recruiting from a known customer base means participants are already familiar with the product, company, or testing process, which reduces onboarding friction and improves the quality of the conversation.

**Tanya Branitska, Market Researcher in the RT3D development software space,** describes progressive profile building as the core advantage:

> “Deep profiling is the real beauty of panels. I can aggregate all of the information we’ve ever gotten from this user, their basic screener, what research they’ve participated in, everything.”

That means that when the research calls for feedback from specific segments, such as those with more or less product familiarity or heavy use of a specific feature, she can connect with customers whose experiences and characteristics align with the needs of the study.

For teams doing longitudinal work, panels are often the only viable option. One ReOps lead at a bioscience organization, for example manages a participant tracker of clinical specialists, a global population of roughly 200–300 people, using year-long consent and automated scheduling to protect a genuinely finite resource. Because their research often depends on repeated engagement with the same population over time, maintaining a panel is less a convenience than a necessity.

The practices of panel management can also lend themselves to small cohorts of longitudinal participants. UX program manager Rachel Wigen-Toccalino, Staff UXR Program Manager in the healthcare industry, ran 6-month engagements with small cohorts of 8–10 people to track how user experience evolved as products were iterated. Using built-in consent forms, having processes for mass outreach, and tracking who has participated recently versus who hasn’t empowered her to cleanly refresh her group of participants for each longitudinal study.

**Case Study: Arya Guinney, Consumer Insights Researcher, National Restaurant Chain**

Arya built her panel using a tool already included in her organization’s existing contract, at no additional cost to the business. The motivation was straightforward: previous research was “inherently anonymous.” The team had tracked customer sentiment scores but had no way to connect them to actual behavior or follow up with the same customers over time. The panel solved for the “why,” enabling the team to engage customers longitudinally and capture video reactions to new concepts. “We wouldn’t be able to tie that to actual behavior and purchase history before, there was no longitudinal nature. Now we can stack what someone says against what they’ve actually done.”

### Cost

DIY recruiting (versus leveraging a third-party panel) is often perceived as the budget-friendly choice. But ad hoc recruiting is rarely “cheap,” at least once you account for labor. User Interviews breaks this down for us: a mid-career professional earning roughly $73/hour USD, manually recruiting 20 B2B participants costs an estimated $4,831 in labor alone. A panel reduces that to around $870 and saves 63 hours of work.

At scale, the gap widens. For teams recruiting 100 qualitative participants per month, panel-based approaches cost between $5,580 and $16,452 less than ad hoc methods.

Beyond costs for labor and time, panels eliminate the procurement drag that makes research irrelevant by the time it’s complete. As Prama Ayalasomayajula, UX Researcher for a B2B CRM, noted, traditional vendor procurement processes can take 60–90 days, a timeline that doesn’t serve anyone.

**Case Study: Ted Kendall, Market researcher, agency founder, and pioneer in online research panels for consumer goods, packaging, and product development**

Ted was running research for a team at a consumer packaged goods (CPG) company with a limited annual budget and was priced out of the large studies his counterparts were commissioning for upwards of $80,000. By building an internal panel, and making it feel human, with consistent communication and a familiar face behind every email, he brought per-survey costs down to roughly $1,200 and cut turnaround from the industry standard of four weeks to eleven days from brief to final report. In six months, the team tested 200 ideas. Response rates hit 50–75%, largely because Ted consistently closed the loop: participants could see exactly how their input changed the product.

### Diversity and Hard-to-Reach Audiences

Ideally, panels give researchers deliberate control over whose voices they hear. Rather than relying on whoever responds to a generic recruitment post, panel managers can intentionally recruit underrepresented groups, target participants by behavior or geography, and avoid over-indexing on the loudest or most engaged voices. For niche professional audiences such as developers, C-suite executives, or clinical specialists, a pre-vetted panel can be especially valuable to reach the right participants quickly and consistently.

**Case Study: Katie Encabo, Client Success Manager in Market & UX Research Consulting**

Katie once managed the build-out of an opt-in research panel for a regional government initiative operating across multiple counties. Their goal wasn’t speed or cost savings, it was representation. “Their purpose was inclusivity and giving constituents an opportunity to participate in government decisions.” The panel was designed to be as open and accessible as possible. They recruited through local events, intercept surveys at venues like the zoo, and grassroots outreach including billboards and table tents. The value proposition to participants wasn’t cash, it was civic voice: be the first to know what’s being planned and weigh in before decisions are made. The panel took two to three years to reach a scale where the data could be trusted to reflect the regional population, but the payoff was a platform that made representation a structural feature of the research process, not an afterthought.

### PII, Compliance, and Legal Drivers

For many researchers, a panel isn’t just operationally useful. It can also provide structure for managing consent and participant data responsibly. Spreadsheets may become difficult to manage as requirements around opt-outs, consent records, deletion requests, and participant data grow more complex. In regulated industries, the risk of non-compliance is a direct cost. Senior Research Operations Specialist Emily Wever made the case for her panel tool by highlighting the financial risks of non-compliance: “Spending a little money on a proper tool now saves a ton compared to regulatory scrutiny later.” In B2B environments, formal opt-in panels can also provide clearer processes for documenting consent and establishing how and when researchers are permitted to contact users.

**Case Study: Shri Amarnath,** Senior UX Research Ops Specialist in the software space, deals with highly sensitive Personally Identifiable Information (PII) at a software company handling payroll and benefits

Amarnath collaborated with legal from the very beginning to establish formalized terms and conditions for their branded panel, the “Dayforce Research Group”. Because their data processing agreements strictly limited direct contact with certain end-users, these early conversations helped establish clear guardrails for what they could and couldn’t do. For example, the legal team mandated that they cannot contact a single individual more than four times a year, a rule Amarnath actively programmed into their panel tool (Ethnio) to automate compliance and prevent accidental over-contacting.

Shri emphasizes the importance of doing this early.

> “Those initial conversations that we had with the legal team to establish formalized terms and conditions really helped us set up guardrails.”

### When NOT to Build a Panel

Panels aren’t the right answer for every organization. Several practitioners were direct about the conditions that make them a poor fit: a lack of an existing customer base or research demand too low to justify the investment. ResearchOps community board member Benson Low was blunt: “If people don’t even value ResearchOps, that’s a very, very hard start.”

Veteran researcher Douglas Wicks, Senior Research Manager and Consultant across the healthcare, telecommunications, and financial services sectors, was equally candid: “Owning is an expense. If this isn’t your forte, is it worth making it your forte? Can you keep it going for more than six months?”

The failure mode most often described isn’t a bad launch, it’s a quiet fade: a panel that gets built, has a short run of success, then goes silent and loses members before it ever proves its sustained value.

**When to Choose Which Strategy**

-   **Build if:** You have the operational capacity to manage the database (more on that here _\[editor: hi readers, we will link when we publish this\]_ and either conduct frequent, iterative research with your own customer base or need consistent access to specific audiences (like your competitors’ customers or niche power users).
-   **Buy if:** You have one-off research needs, lack sufficient staffing for operating the panel, lack needed processes to adequately handle customer data, or need to reach a broad, representative consumer base outside your current ecosystem.
