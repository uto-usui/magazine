### **Say Hello to Meta-UX/UI**

With forewarnings of UX’s death, businesses moving to darker practices, and tech’s total submission to AI, UX/UI needs to intentionally adapt to survive.

As we see it, there is a way out, and it is a way that reunites us with the very core of our mission as researchers and designers.

As a Researcher-Designer turned UX Strategist-Research and Design Firm Co-Founder-Thought Leader with 103 sprint cycles under my belt, I am excited to present what our growing community of researchers and designers spread across 15 countries has dubbed “the next big iteration of UX/UI:” **meta-UX/UI**.

_Rather than designing products,_ **_meta-UX/UI is research and design on the UX/UI research and design process itself._**

We seek to discover and define the collective needs and values of researchers and designers to redesign UX/UI processes. Our deliverables take the form of UX/UI tools, methodologies, and frameworks that explicitly embed designers’ values. Our goal is to operationalize our values and measurably increase the adoption of “Good” (as a metric) under the business.

### The Good Position of ReOps

I had a shocking realization last year: the most important phenomenon in UX/UI has no name.

A colleague asked me to adapt a deliverable to a “less granular form for stakeholders.”

The insights didn’t change. The direction for the final deliverable didn’t change. Only the story did.

It pulled on the heart

In favor of the user —

A deep resonance.

This work isn’t explicitly named in our job descriptions. This work isn’t paid. This work falls under the shadows of “impact.”

In that moment, I am not only surfacing design recommendations but designing the deliverable itself. Design on design.

When we redesign a design deliverable or break a UX design aid to better fit our organizational needs, we are doing meta-UX/UI.

This work doesn’t just intervene at the level of design and delivery.

Meta-UX/UI can redesign tools, methodologies, and frameworks at the level of discovery.

The fish doesn’t see water.

But once it does, it understands how it could leverage meta-UX/UI to better align our values with the pressures and incentives of the systems we work in.

Instead of erring on the side of stakeholders’ needs,

> **What if we made room for our values as designers in the research and design process under the business?**

### Our Values: What Makes Meta-UX/UI Different

Disciplines that seek to build better worlds often call upon accreditation bodies to set the standards for how to achieve good. Doctors have the Hippocratic Oath. Lawyers have the Bar Association’s Model Rules of Professional Conduct. Even the Institutional Review Board (IRB) has a strict process for working with human subjects.

While we aren’t doctors, lawyers, or scientists, design intentionally seeks to move people — diverting attention, instilling a need, guiding them towards interactions that are deemed valuable. Outside of the business, many of us want to create online worlds that are safer, serendipitous, and fun.

So much of our work is a negotiation. While we can (and do) alter our methods and frameworks to meet the demands of the project, we believe that meta-UX/UI is not about changing frameworks and methods to pad bottom lines. It is about ensuring that our values are not extracted from the frameworks and methods that we use.

#### **This is a big call.**

It requires us to give shape to our collective values as researchers and designers. Since the birth of our discipline, we have circled this question: what are our core values outside of those guiding design? As advocates for building better worlds, what values do we call upon to construct these realities?

In the absence of a central accrediting agency, our Thought Leaders, bootcamps, and corporations have filled the space. In my interrogation, I continuously stumbled upon the same three guiding principles:

(1) Be objective,

(2) Lead with empathy, and

(3) “Do right by the user.”

When held up to other ethical guidelines, these three principles are not very robust. Placed next to each other, they start to even seem contradictory, with objectivity seeming at odds with empathy and emotion. Some researchers and designers try to wave away some of these frustrations by simply advocating that “doing right by the user” is sufficient alone, which seems to squash complexity and is duly lacking in rigor.

Theory is different from practice, though. We aren’t pure theorists, and what seems confusing in theory feels different in practice.

**I am a designer after all. And while I employ these values, I feel I have a deeper understanding of what our principles attempt to get at in practice.**

> Our research and design [is about making room to allow the person in front of us to express themselves as fully as possible, trying to capture as much of them as possible, so we can act in ways that uphold their values.](https://midstlabs.substack.com/p/empathy-is-all-wrong-against-empathy?r=4vjp90)

At least to me, this is the mindset and ethos that I feel I am embodying when I actually do my work.

While users and businesses called for simplicity, efficiency, and seamless user experiences, we built the white box hierarchies we now find ourselves trapped in. There were hundreds of ways to design for user needs, but with the precedent set by Big Tech, we chose minimalism and mimicry. Big Design chose to become increasingly less visible, smaller grids, taking away, always less, yet always intentionally moving users to the business’s desired outcomes.

**Design has always been about movement; it is not objective or distant. It is intentional, and a designer makes choices.**

With the spirit and vigor with which we weigh and choose hues of blue, why are we barred from saying how these choices manifest outside of aesthetics? The user is not the sole guide, just as the business is not. Why can’t we make room for ourselves in this interaction, acknowledge our presence and choice? In the end, aesthetics and usability are inextricable.

I want more for the user than what the business wants, but if I can sell the business on what the user and I want, maybe we can get more of what _we_ want.

### Operationalizing our Values

MIdST LABS is prototyping and exploring early methods for how researchers and designers can operationalize their values into their work. We offer two directions for what meta-UX/UI may look like.

#### **Prototype: “How Might We…If…” Question**

“How Might We…” questions are well known in the industry because they succinctly capture user needs while making design actionable; we repurposed the [“How Might We…” question into the “How Might We…If…” question](https://midstlabs.substack.com/p/the-new-how-might-we-question).

> **_How might we_** _\+ \_\_\_do what\_\_\_ +_ **_for_** _+ \_\_\_whom\_\_\_+_ **_in order to_** _+ \_\_\_\_benefit, gain, result\_\_\_\_ +_ **_if_** _\+ \_\_\_speculation\_\_\_?_

By affixing “if” at the end of the question, we ensure that the question is still actionable and user-directed, while making room for values-driven solutioning. This technique draws from speculative design, which [has long flirted with UX/UI](https://uxdesign.cc/stealing-from-the-future-with-speculative-design-e769059b6689). Speculative design is a design thinking method that seeks to challenge how the present looks by imagining alternatives of the world.

This prototype is an early iteration, but it offers a starting point that is actually useful and actionable for the industry designer while allowing us to envision different alternatives to our design.

#### Direction: More than Human Design

UX/UI is in the user experience and user interface business. While our work is exclusively with end users and business representatives, it is becoming harder to ignore the impact of our solutions on other beings. From [AI data centers that are draining community water supplies](https://www.nytimes.com/2025/07/14/technology/meta-data-center-water.html) to [interactions that set single-use plastics as the default](https://totalfood.com/cutoutcutlery-campaign-saves-millions-of-plastics-from-ending-up-in-our-oceans/#:~:text=In%202019%2C%20Los%20Angeles%2Dbased,entering%20the%20environment%20every%20month.), our research never includes those that fall outside of the paying customer, unless we’re interested in conversion.

As researchers and designers, we likely care deeply about the other beings our work impacts. But for the purist UX designer who believes other beings are out of scope, it seems essential to address concerns that disrupt end users’ worlds and the use of our products in those worlds.

![Sample UX persona of Rosa Cho providing details “About Rosa,” “Behavioral Considerations,” “Frustrations,” “Goals,” and “Tasks” to aid a designer in the design process.](https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/720/1*yEFxTekN2v6AeczYbhTeUg.png)

Sample UX persona that many of us use daily in our work (All credit to [NN/G](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/persona/).)

Personas have long been used to capture users’ motivations and behaviors in a way that emphasizes their humanity. Jeroen Spoelstra’s [non-human personas](https://www.unbeatenstudio.com/nonhumanpersona-deepdive) and Monica Snzel’s [actant mapping canvas](https://uxdesign.cc/tools-for-environment-centered-designers-actant-mapping-canvas-a495df19750e) are tools that sought to extend the persona framework to capture the needs of others outside of humans. Spoelstra used the persona method to capture the motivations and behaviors of non-humans, and Snzel captured how we may be able to design around the needs and motivations arising from relationships instead of individuals.

![A persona of “Trees” highlighting the trees’ challenges and needs in the manner of a traditional UX persona.](https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*tmN9vSRSjyp4ineHb0aN8w.jpeg)

A sample Non-Human Persona mimicking many of the characteristics found on traditional UX personas. (All credit to [Monika Sznel, Jeroen Spoelstra, Martin Tomitsch, and Damien Lutz](https://uxdesign.cc/non-human-and-non-user-personas-for-life-centred-design-c34d5ddb78f).)

Outside of Spoelstra and Snzel’s work, the inclusion of other beings as stakeholders has been largely confined to academia. Academia, while offering valuable alternatives, often ignores the difficulty of actually working under a business that solely cares about profit. Designers are not granted the right to just throw their hands up and not comply. It requires creativity in order to balance profit maximization with Good, making the solutions far more situational.

![Three concentric circles are divided in half to allow “Problems,” “Direct Actants,” and “Indirect Actants” to be filled in for Human and Non-Human Actants, outlining the interconnected nature of problems.](https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*1apVm46uVNPzW0dqCz60rg.png)

Actant Mapping Canvas Template (All credit to [Monika Sznel](https://uxdesign.cc/tools-for-environment-centered-designers-actant-mapping-canvas-a495df19750e).)

While we have some [grievances with the non-human persona](https://midstlabs.substack.com/p/empathy-is-all-wrong-against-empathy), we see the value in these early iterations and are actively working to make them more viable under the business.

To motivate this work, MIdST LABS has posed:

> **_Primary How Might We #1.1:_**

> _How might we create actionable, flexible, and simple tools and frameworks for researchers and designers to support a range of beings if we sought to not be reliant upon sameness in the form of empathy and fitting complex beings into boxes for humans?_

> **_Secondary How Might We #2:_**

> _How might we present findings and outcomes to executives to appeal to the prioritization of profit maximization if we intend not to betray our expanded understanding of the collective good?_

All of this offers a huge opportunity for those working in the field to meaningfully contribute to this work, and this is the very essence of what meta-UX/UI seeks to do. It will require those in industry to tackle this alongside business constraints in order to ensure viability.

### Conclusion

Meta-UX/UI seeks to bake values into the redesign of UX/UI. This work will be incredibly pivotal as we face the mass adoption of AI, as this is the very [cornerstone of the development of aligned AI.](https://www.quantamagazine.org/what-does-it-mean-to-align-ai-with-human-values-20221213/)

Research Operations specialists hold a special position in doing this work, as they are already masters of our two worlds. While they work under business constraints, their allegiance lies with users; their knowledge can stand to strengthen methodologies and frameworks to meet business needs without sacrificing their commitments to users.

So, for the research operations specialist, we leave you with:

> **How might we build new methodologies and frameworks that better align with the values and vision that guide the user-centered researcher and designer, without being compromised, yet aligned with the business?**
