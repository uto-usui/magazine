#### Understanding and mapping B2B customer journey

This article follows my previous one [where I described B2B journeys as living ecosystems](https://medium.com/design-bootcamp/the-goal-is-the-journey-itself-3f945e3d0c3c) that are constantly changing. Based on this theoretical framework, I prepared an execution plan in our team, and this is what the content of the next article will be. I will present you with a step-by-step framework of how I proceeded when mapping the customer journey in five countries with multiple actors.

I would like to emphasize that the following framework represents a **qualitative approach** to the issue. Personally, this procedure helped me to reveal patterns of cooperation, how our buyers perceive us as a team and as a product, and of course — what is good and what is bad.

**What this framework delivered:**

-   A single, visual journey map across five countries that made complexity legible
-   Clear visibility into universal vs. country-specific pain points (via the heatmap)
-   A service blueprint that exposed handoffs, bottlenecks, and ownership gaps in the request flow
-   Context to interpret signals (UX maturity) and sentiments (Needscope) fairly across teams
-   An opportunity backlog tied to owners, priorities, and impact — ready for planning
-   A list of blind spots to prevent false certainty and guide follow-up research

#### Our context

To put the story in perspective — I work as a researcher in a team of designers who create a design system for an international bank, and we are all employees of the bank, not an external design firm. Our developers are also an internal team. The design system has been around for four years and has been gradually built based on the needs and requirements of the countries — our buyers. Countries — buyers — either have their own internal team of designers who create final applications, or they don’t. The same goes for developers who create final applications used by bank clients.

So this is our initial mess that we needed to untangle, map, understand, and — if a higher power (stakeholders) allows — also effectively solve.

#### Phase 1 — Preparations

After the project was announced, I was given enough time to prepare, and within three weeks I went from “_nothing_” to creating a scenario for internal interviews based on the requirements of our team leads.

**Important** — discuss with your lead that you really need this time for preparation because there is a lot. You can use the argument that “the professional literature agrees,” that there is objectively a lot, and you need enough time for preparation and planning.

After I found out what was waiting for me, I could continue with **stakeholder interviews**, during which we talked about expectations and assumptions. I analyzed the interviews and prepared the **goals**, basic **assumptions** that we want to verify, and we also defined the **target group.**

The goal was influenced by a prosaic fact — **we needed the results as soon as possible.** In addition to the design system itself, our team also offers a bunch of other products (for example, research), but mapping them all would be extremely lengthy, and the number of roles that we would have to cover would also increase. This means more dedicated time for interviews and more time for analysis. The leads, therefore, agreed that we would focus on the core part — how our buyers use the design system (libraries, documentation, etc.) and how they request new components. That was the **first narrowing of the scope**.

The next one followed when defining the **target group**. Given the goal, out of all the countries, we selected for this mapping only those countries that have an internal team of designers and developers who prepare their final applications. So, from 10 countries, we became 5. We also discussed the actors who appear in the journey. For this mapping, we decided to leave out decision-makers, stakeholders, CX people, business architects, and researchers. And we focused only on how designers and developers in selected countries use the design system.

Another problem that arose during thinking and preparations was data **analysis**. How will we evaluate the claims of individual designers? Based on what will we assess their impact? Here, the [NN/g **UX maturity survey**](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-maturity-model/) came to the rescue, for a contextual understanding of individual teams and subsequent interpretation of their pain points. It would be very wrong to assume that all departments in individual countries are at the same level. The second tool was the [**Needscope survey** modeled after Kantar](https://www.kantar.com/en-cn/expertise/brand-growth/brand-purpose-and-positioning/needscope). I will get into it more in detail later in the text.

Our tangle has unraveled a bit.

![Needscope segments by Kantar model. Segments are divided by axis extrover-introvert and collective-individualism](https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*0ulcefrGKyC9yJBCfaJGxQ.jpeg)

#### Phase 2: Internal interviews

Based on our goal and assumptions from stakeholder interviews, I prepared a script for interviews with senior designers in our team. Why? **Because I am not a designer and I don’t understand it.** And if I were to talk directly to designers and developers in the countries, I wouldn’t ask them the right questions. My capacity was limited to “So you use Figma, right?”

But our seniors educated me enough and broadened my horizons. We talked about how the design system works, what makes it up, how it is used, what is the content of our Figma files. At the same time, **we balanced this technical side of things with a discussion about our buyers.** What is their experience with them? What are our relationships? What are their most common problems?

**Important** — as I mentioned in my first article — B2B is cyclical, so we can very likely assume that the problems mentioned are the biggest problems, because they repeat themselves over and over, increase frustration, or escalate into resignation and apathy. I also approached the problems that our buyers named to me in the same way.

I analyzed the interviews with our team and **prepared** five **hypotheses** that included aspects such as t**echnical, product,** and **relationship**. In addition, I also obtained the unique value proposition of our team from the interviews. Through a personal perspective and deep understanding of the product, the designers summarized for us what our product is, why it is beneficial as a business idea, and thanks to what specific parameters we can say that it is very mature and highly professional. T**hese observations very nicely illustrated how different the value proposition of a designer is for other designers, compared to the standard corporate KPI-driven paragraph**.

While preparing the deliverables, we debated whether to prepare a **service blueprint**, but in the end we rejected it as too much. But as it turned out, we needed it. At least I needed it **to understand what was connected to what, how internal processes work, how communications work and how it affects the product.** Thanks to the blueprint, I knew all the touchpoints and connections, my questions for the interviews were more precise and even if the interview turned in a different direction, I had my process map and knew how to return to where we needed to.

At this stage, we also sent a **UX maturity survey** to individual teams in the countries, via MS Forms. The surveys were anonymous and to avoid confusion about who sent what, I created 5 identical forms that differed in the language of the welcome, depending on the country they were going to. As I mentioned, we drew from the standardized NNg form. **We changed a few terms to fit our context**, such as including terms like experience department, bank environment, etc.

**Interesting** — in one country, the chief officer for experience design responded to our survey with the explanation that his answer was a representative sample for the entire department, because he has the best overview of the state of UX maturity of his team. Well, such things happen, we thanked him for the answer and accepted it.

#### Phase three — Interviews with buyers

Over two weeks, our intern Andrej Vancák and I conducted interviews with 14 designers and 9 developers. As I mentioned in the introduction, this was a qualitative study. Again, for a prosaic reason — the total number of designers in the countries and the **total number** of developers **is too low to consider a quantitative study.** And even if we somehow magically (_or coercively_) obtained answers from all of them.  
In the interviews, we followed the mentioned blueprint and the semi-structured interview script. As in the internal interviews, in the interviews with the countries, **we combined technical, product** and **relationship aspects.** At the same time, we asked the participants how they would imagine solutions to the problems they told us about. Thanks to these **suggestions**, we collected a lot of opportunities that serve as inputs for planning for the following quarters.

During the interviews, we forwarded the **Needscope survey** to the individual teams. Why? As we learned from the theoretical level of the B2B customer journey, **emotional and relational aspects are extremely important and directly influence the journey.** The second reason is that we needed a deeper context — how our buyers and counterparts in the countries perceive us. From historical experience, we know that **it is very difficult for research participants to verbalize such emotional-driven understandings**. Therefore, we chose the projection technique modeled after Kantar’s Needscope as a data collection method.

During collaborative workshop _(coffee+croissants+FigJam)_ the whole team prepared the individual segments for the Needscope matrix and the subsequent cards with the visuals. I divided them into two groups. One prepared the visuals for the segments on the Extrovert — Collective — Introvert pages and the other group prepared the visuals for the segments falling under Extrovert — Individualism — Introvert.

Since it was a visual survey, we did not want to use MS Forms, because it does not offer a good display. Therefore, we chose the Typeform tool. **Participants could click through the images, look at them, think about them, and choose one that represented our team** (social dimension) and one that presented **our product.**

#### Phase 4 — Data analysis

This is where the fun begins. Approximately 24 hours of recorded interviews and almost 70 complete questionnaires.

Dear colleagues, do you remember the dark ages before AI? When we had to analyze it for a month through sticky notes, markers and whiteboards? Literally stone age.

Thanks to **internal AI tools**, I was able to process this amount of data relatively quickly. I went **country by country** and only then did I start looking for patterns, looking for correlations and formulating general conclusions.

And yes, AI tools are great, but still the most time-consuming part of my time was reading the analyses it generated for me. Why? Besides my paranoia, the main problem was that our internal **AI tools are not fed with data that would help them evaluate situations in the context** of our team, in the context of relationships, in the context of processes, in the context of some event that happened two years ago that we know is essential for the product.

So how did I proceed? First of all, I **formulated the prompts very carefully**. Through trial and error, I came up with formulations that generated the answers I needed. In the end, I ended up with 14 prompts, which I **entered separately for each country.** Then came the reading.

Yes, it is true that the answers were repeated to a certain extent, but through each prompt I looked at the **issues from a different perspective**. If I had only proceeded with “_Summarize this” — “What are the results based on hypotheses?”- “What are the recommendations?”_ **I would lose a lot of data, the results would be devoid of nuances, and it is very likely that I would also be blind to behavioral patterns and correlations.**

**Important** — Yes, you should use AI for analysis, but you have to use your cognitive research abilities, your own understanding of your context and your knowledge about the project to gain relevant results.

Then I started with deliverables:

-   Customer journey map as a central artifact
-   Customer journey presentation by countries
-   Pain points heatmap
-   Opportunity backlog
-   Service blueprint for main process of interest
-   Executive summary

#### Conclusion

B2B journeys are living ecosystems. Treating them that way — scoping tightly, honoring context, and balancing technical, product, and relational lenses — made the mapping meaningful and actionable. The combination of a **qualitative core** (interviews, blueprinting, Needscope) and careful, **human-guided AI analysi**s gave us both depth and speed without losing nuance.  
Most importantly, it created a shared language between our design system team and country teams, turning recurring frustrations into concrete improvement work.

If you try a variant of this approach — or if you see gaps — let me know. The map is living, so is the conversation.
