The recent downturn has revealed an unpleasant truth — Research Operations teams and the researchers within are often the first to be cut when corporate cost cutters swing the axe.

Yet there are several things research operations leads and researchers can to avoid heads rolling, but there is no one size fits all to avoid it.

That is the advice of [Kate Towsey, author of _Research that Scales_](https://rosenfeldmedia.com/books/research-that-scales/), and one of the founding voices of the ResearchOps community.

I spoke to her recently, asking her about her thoughts on how ResearchOps has developed and what the future holds. In the first of two articles I’ll focus on how she has seen ResearchOps be implemented while in the second it will be Kate’s thoughts on what the future holds.

## Why ResearchOps is often the first on the chopping block

Research operations are typically a cost centre to organisations. They pay for a research operations lead, researchers, incentives, studies… Yet what do they bring in?

As [Kate](https://katetowsey.com/) said, “Researchers face job instability. With only 11% confident about their future in research, the outlook is bleak.”

So how can we get around it? Kate’s advice focuses on understanding organisational goals, proving value, and hiring strategically.

First, Kate is clear that it is important is to understand what the organisation’s goals are and to engage the key stakeholders and decision makers.

“In 2019 at Atlassian, I was considering expanding headcount to focus primarily on researchers. But I realised it would leave us vulnerable if we couldn’t demonstrate our value.

“My leader advised that while it was important to look after researchers, we also needed to include product managers in our scope. It was about demonstrating value across a wide range of individuals.”

This leads to growth across teams (horizontal) and leadership influence (vertical).

Second, it was to not just engage them but find out what they cared about and that these vary across organisations:

> “When discussing top metrics, people often desire a definitive list, but the best metrics are those that resonate with the organisation’s priorities.

“At Atlassian, ‘customer closeness’ was a key term, reflecting how much time Atlassians spent with customers. Alternatively, focusing on efficiency could demonstrate cost savings from tool changes, aligning with organisational goals while achieving the same level of closeness at a cheaper price.”

While her team still lost a couple of researchers, others lost 50% or more of their team.

She reassures that you don’t need the CEO’s ear in order to discover these priorities but to use your researcher skills to listen and observe. Most organisations set out their priorities regularly, such as at the start of a financial year — make sure that research operations outputs are aligned.

This leads onto Kate’s next set of recommendations — making the benefit of having a research team be widely known throughout an organisation.

## How to demonstrate ResearchOps’ value

As part of listening it is important to then act and show value for where the organisation is looking to achieve goals.

With many organisations taking a metric-heavy approach to success, is making ResearchOps get more involved with data the answer?

“Some teams are employing ResearchOps to develop teams leveraging data and insights to ensure that insight generation is core to customer interaction,” said Kate.

She went to add that “necessitates a collaboration between strategic visionaries and operations professionals with profound research knowledge, blending strategy with practical implementations.”

However — and this is for the next article — she adds that AI is being used to support, not replace , the process to use data more effectively, while ensuring that qualitative insights are emphasised over quantitative ones.

“Finance teams appreciate prudent spending. I’d ask where the money will go in the upcoming financial year — for example, into accessibility. If we fail to recognise this, we might face repercussions. By aligning with the head of accessibility, I was able to secure four headcounts for it. This includes conducting research and handling consent forms and incentives.”

“Understanding leadership priorities doesn’t require being in their pockets but being perceptive to financial signals.”

You also need support, research operations can’t succeed on its own: “You need support. Partner with the stakeholders to learn the language is a good first step.”

As part of that partnership, researchers and research operations professionals need to educate others in an organisation about what the role is.

> “ResearchOps is not just about building research libraries, it’s about orchestrating diverse skills, determining real organisational needs.

“In some cases, an expert temporarily enhances foundational structures. Ongoing flexibility requires balancing expertise, strategy, and administration, ensuring daily operations thrive beyond sheer maintenance.”

By doing this it can help with the final key to helping research operations teams succeed — by knowing what a research team does, it means that organisations can hire correctly.

## Helping orgs hire ResearchOps teams properly

For Kate, what lay at the heart of many issues organisations have with research operations is that it was not set up properly, so the team was not set up to succeed.

Part of this is the organisation’s poor understanding of what a research team consists of, and this is where help is needed.

> “The emergence of ResearchOps has unfortunately led some to perceive it merely as a job title, wherein hiring someone equates to a completed task.”

Kate feels that when recruiting, non-research operations stakeholders feel that a researcher can do everything.

“Hiring a design manager or researcher is mistakenly thought to encapsulate all necessary functions. It’s a fallacy to confine these roles to a single person’s capabilities; operations function as a dynamic system and impact organisational habits daily.”

Making sure that hiring managers knew the reality of what a researcher can and can’t do is also important:

> “It’s key that organisations are realistic about hiring. Too many think that one person can do multiple tasks.”

As Kate told me, even with her team of 12, she saw job descriptions that combined all 12 of her teams’ skills in one, which was completely unrealistic for a mid-level ResearchOps coordinator (or most people for that matter).

Indeed, Kate goes on to say that some organisations feel that one researcher can be a Swiss army knife: “librarian, financier, procurement handler, research panels’ manager, and community leader. This approach typically doesn’t hire strategically, leading to junior-level roles with firefighting capabilities but lacking broader strategy.

“If misunderstood as an expensive outlay without establishing scalable systems, operations flounder.”

To overcome these problems, existing researchers should support and engage senior strategists capable of defining and executing broad initiatives.

## Summary

Whether you’re establishing a researcher, operations team or you lead an existing one, the Kate’s key advice is to engage. Use your research skills to listen, understand and act on what you learn.

In turbulent times, ResearchOps teams must be proactive, it must not merely survive, but thrive. Kate’s mantra says it all: ‘Ride it like you stole it”.

Which she says means “abandon outdated attitudes and embrace creativity”.
