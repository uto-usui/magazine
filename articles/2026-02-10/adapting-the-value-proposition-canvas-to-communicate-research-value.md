Working in UX has given me the opportunity to collaborate closely with teams across an organization to design and launch products. Along the way, I’ve learned some of the approaches my cross-functional colleagues use in their work. One such approach is the Value Proposition Canvas, a classic tool that helps teams define the unique value a product brings to a specific customer. Years later after learning about this tool, I found a unique way to adapt it to explain the value of research.

Not only have I worked closely with other functions, I’ve also led teams across marketing communications, engineering, and product in addition to UX. These experiences have deepened my empathy for UX partners. I’ve come to appreciate the unique value each function brings and the magic that happens when they work well together within a system. That drive has often led me to create frameworks and tools that improve the workplace environment, enable effective collaboration, and act as a mediator bringing teams together. So when I saw research and other functions struggling to understand and connect with each other, I adapted the Value Proposition Canvas to better communicate the value of research.

## Differences in Goals are Inevitable

Let’s face it: _Researchers_ and _stakeholders_ often don’t speak the same language and have different project and personal career goals. Stakeholders such as executives, engineers, product managers and marketing may be focused more on delivering value quickly, meeting customer requests, and/or hitting financial targets.

Researchers, on the other hand, value rigor, methodical approaches, and deeply understanding user problems. While both sides provide value in different ways to do what’s best for the product, these differing priorities and perspectives can easily create friction.

This misalignment can result in researchers being sidelined, brought in at the last minute with prescriptive tasks, or pressured to move quickly at the cost of craftsmanship and user needs. This can lead to frustration, burnout, and a feeling of being undervalued.

So, how do we break this cycle? The key is effective communication. If we can clearly explain the unique value of UX research — tailored to the needs of each stakeholder — we can bridge the gap and pave the way for better collaboration.

## Putting the Value Proposition Canvas to Work

![Value Proposition Canvas by Blank, Christensen, Godin, Pigneur, Osterwalder](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/0*ltnGqD3bEfMl9u_r)

By using the Value Proposition Canvas, you’ll not only map the research team’s strengths but also consider the needs and goals of stakeholders. This approach helps researchers communicate their value in a way that resonates with each stakeholder partner, whether that’s a designer needing immediate guidance on designs or a product manager making the right prioritization decisions.

The first step is to understand the goals of all parties involved. Once we do that, we can map those to the strengths of the research team and create tailored value propositions. This ensures that when you communicate, you’re speaking directly to your stakeholders’ needs. All it takes is a couple hours workshopping with the team to start leveraging the benefits of this tool.

Let’s break down each of the steps:

### 1\. Identify Stakeholders and Their Needs (approx. 45–60 min)

Start by identifying the key stakeholders you work with. These could include:

-   Designers
-   Product Managers
-   Marketing Teams
-   Upper Management/Executives
-   Engineers

Next, narrow down the list to the stakeholders who would benefit most from this method. I recommend aiming for your top 3 stakeholder groups. For each of those groups, create a Value Proposition Canvas, starting with defining their _pain points_, _gains_, and _jobs_. Create stickies for each item and place them in the appropriate section.

To generate items, ask yourself the following questions.

-   **Pain Points**: What keeps them up at night? What challenges or obstacles are they facing when trying to do their job?
-   **Gains**: What outcomes do they care about? What positive outcomes are they seeking — better products, faster decision-making, data to prove they made the right decision, results to prove they deserve a promotion, insights to support strategy?
-   **Jobs**: What are their core goals or responsibilities? What is expected of them?
-   Consider their functional, emotional, and social jobs. For example, a functional job for a babysitting service could be to help parents easily find quality, reliable care nearby. An emotional job might be to reduce parents’ stress. Lastly, a social job could be to impress their manager with innovative solutions.

### 2\. Map Your Team’s Capabilities to Their Needs (approx. 30 min.)

Now that you’ve outlined stakeholder needs, it’s time to think about your team’s strengths. What skills or capabilities does your research team bring to the table that could meet their needs? For example:

-   Conducting evaluations
-   Running continuous discovery
-   Analyzing user behavior
-   Recruiting the right users
-   Designing experiments
-   Storytelling
-   Negotiating
-   Assessing accessibility
-   etc

List all your skills and capabilities. Then, match them to the pain points and gains of your stakeholders by placing them on the canvases of each stakeholder. Which of your team’s strengths will help each group overcome their challenges? What value will your research bring to the table to help them meet their goals?

### 3\. Create Tailored Value Propositions (approx. 15 min.)

Now we’re ready to craft value proposition statements that speak directly to the needs of each stakeholder. Each proposition should be succinct, targeted, and clear about how UX research can make their work easier or more impactful. Look at the pains, gains, and jobs for each stakeholder partner and the capabilities and skills you listed. Then, create a statement that represents your unique value proposition for that stakeholder partner. You will use these statements as a reminder and a framework for crafting key talking points of your communications with that partner. For example:

**A Designer needs:**

-   A research partner who can provided fast, actionable insights preferably a sprint ahead to:
-   Save them time, give them confidence and autonomy to make informed design decisions — without slowing down the process.
-   Provide evidence to help the team align and avoid lengthy discussions.
-   **You might say:** “Let’s run a quick click test so you can get the results in a couple days before you meet the team.”

**A Product Manager needs**:

-   A research partner who can provide quantifiable insights to:
-   Feed prioritization formulas, inform the roadmap and backlog and communicate valid reasons to the team
-   Provide evidence/metrics to impress executives solution will be successful and thereby stand out to management
-   **You might act by:** Including data that can easily fit into a product management prioritization framework as part of your research executive summary. For example, if your Product Management team uses the RICE (Reach, Impact, Confidence, and Effort) formula to decide what initiatives to prioritize, you can provide data that fits into those four areas.

**Upper Management and executives need**:

-   A research partner who can deliver brief, easily digestible, and actionable insights to:
-   Best prioritize short-term and long-term efforts, budget, and resources across the organization
-   Inform future direction, build their confidence in their 3–5 year strategy, and hit target business goals
-   **You might act by:** Creating a 16:9 infographic highlighting top findings related to each business objective and showing short-term and long-term impact. This infographic could then be reused in business-related presentations

Tailoring these unique value propositions ensures you’re addressing the specific concerns and goals of each stakeholder, highlighting how research benefits them in the most effective ways, rather than simply focusing on what you feel is most important and hoping they’ll listen.

## Take It a Step Further with a Communications Plan

A communications plan template

Now that you’ve identified stakeholder partners and crafted your value propositions, it’s time to ensure your research communication hits the mark. A communications plan can help you plan regular communication of your unique value to stakeholders, in the right way.

In addition to tailored messaging, there are a few other strategies that, when combined, can be effective:

-   **Tailored Messaging**: Craft specific messages for each stakeholder group, addressing their unique pain points and showing how your research provides solutions.
-   **General Messaging**: Provide a broad overview of your research team’s capabilities and the overall value they bring to the organization.
-   **Timed Messaging**: Share insights at key moments in the product lifecycle — such as before major launches, after usability tests, or following user feedback sessions.

Tools for the types of messaging above can include:

-   Team meetings (1:1s, stakeholder alignment meetings, report readouts, etc)
-   Videos that introduce the research team, highlight key findings of studies, demo user issues and pain points, and highlight successful outcomes.
-   Email newsletters with updates of ongoing research, links to reports, and upcoming opportunities for collaboration. Use this as a way to expose others to different types of methods to mitigate prescriptive requests.
-   Larger get-togethers like lunch and learns, all-hands, and quarterly readouts showcasing your most impactful and high-interest research. Use this opportunity to show you understand what the business priorities are and how you are collaborating with and helping teams make the best decisions.

## Final Thoughts & Outcomes

At the end of the day, aligning your research team’s goals with those of your stakeholders is essential for building strong partnerships and effective collaboration. If UX research isn’t communicating its value clearly, the risk of wasted time, misaligned goals, and ultimately, missed opportunities is high.

By using the Value Proposition Canvas, you can tailor your messaging to address stakeholder pain points and ensure your team is seen as an integral part of the decision-making process. The benefits are clear:

-   Increased demand for UX research
-   More effective and impactful research requests
-   Stronger collaboration and improved outcomes

This approach fosters empathy and understanding between teams, allowing everyone to see how research supports their goals and making sure your team feels appreciated. The result? Stronger relationships, clearer communication, and a shared vision for success.

Try adapting the Value Proposition Canvas in your next project, and see how it can strengthen your relationships!
