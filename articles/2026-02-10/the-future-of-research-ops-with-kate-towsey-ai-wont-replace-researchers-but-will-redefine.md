Artificial Intelligence (AI) is coming to research operations. Yes, “AI is coming to your industry” is a common topic of discussion for every organisation at the moment, but unlike some past fads (remember when everything was going to be crypto?) AI has many eager users in research operations.

This includes Kate Towsey, one of the catalysts of ResearchOps, who is embracing AI using. She uses it to help her work and she’s embarking on a documentary on the subject, so do [check out Kate’s site](https://katetowsey.com/) for when that is available.

Here at ResearchOps we have published [articles on AI](https://medium.com/researchops-community/using-ai-tools-to-augment-user-experience-research-workflows-b10e9bddec9a) and while some in the industry are [sceptical about the role AI will have](https://www.reddit.com/r/UXResearch/comments/1at5md7/how_will_ai_effect_ux_research/), Kate believes that with proper thought to develop its adoption, AI can help research operations teams and people who carry out research.

## Better use of AI means better use of people

[In my last article](https://medium.com/researchops-community/the-first-cut-is-to-researchops-and-how-to-avoid-it-with-kate-towsey-4b86eaccb5b9) I highlighted Kate’s key points on how organisations may perceive “having a research ops lead”as a tickbox exercise with high expectations and low organisational support.

Instead, when supported well, Kate sees ResearchOps as a way to marry data and insights from AI and other sources to enable better understanding of users and insights around them.

“Teams are hiring ResearchOps to build a team and leverage the data and insights to do the research so insight generation can be core to customers.

“What they are asking people to do is enable designers, product owners and others to do good quality research in an easy and efficient way, and to use AI to support that. The next thing is that once enablement is done you can make better use of the data you have and take ownership of insight and knowledge.”

This will help ResearchOps remain relevant, even if it means taking on more responsibilities. But one point that Kate made in the previous article is that ResearchOps can be treated as a Swiss Army Knife that can do just about anything with a small size.

This can add to pressure on teams, and in order to not just survive but to thrive Kate argues that “we need ResearchOps specialists to do better and use people better than we have”.

One way to do that is to farm out repetitive tasks to AI and tools: “What if AI can be used to enhance a team and allow it to focus what they do best, rather than spend time on repetitive tasks?

“The question becomes: Do we need our researchers to spend hours on this? Is it innovative, or could an AI moderator or synthetic user handle these tasks and generate interesting hypotheses?”

## AI as a partner

Kate also believes that bots can be used to support data analysis and report generation — two areas of research that many find time consuming.

“Bots take on different tasks, one might generate ResearchOps reports. This shift isn’t about cutting out human intelligence but about enabling focus on broader objectives.

This means AI can help not just a research operations team but those who work with researchers, as long as the limits and peculiarities are understood.

While insights and report generation is an important part of the researcher role, what about the source of all data in the first place — the users?

Kate believes AI may well affect the foundation of research and our understanding of consumer behaviour. This includes using so-called synthetic users, bots that mimic the behaviour of real users, in order to help with activities such as rapid prototyping.

“There’s a place for synthetic users,” says Kate, “but knowing when and how to deploy the right tool is critical. It’s about choosing the right instrument for the task, treating data with care to foster understanding.”

Kate’s not alone in thinking this. In 2023 [NN Group posted their thoughts on when and where to use synthetic users](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/synthetic-users/) and while NN believes “real user research is essential”, it believes that synthetic users can supplement existing research and can be useful for desk research and generating hypotheses.

## AI spicing up the future of research

Kate’s takeaway on how to include AI in research in the future:

“Like seasoning a curry, it’s about understanding the right mix and applying it to achieve outstanding results at day’s end.”

Kate is an optimist on ResearchOps’ future — she has seen it grow from a new discipline that she played a large part in evangelising, and has showed how it has weathered previous problems.

“ResearchOps has quickly evolved within eight years from aiding researchers to designing organisational knowledge systems. We are now those who design and own these critical skills.

“Throughout this evolution:

1.  Organisations have become more convinced of the necessity for research.
2.  Research technology has developed parallel to product design, empowering research personnel beyond recruitment within new technological landscapes.
3.  GDPR’s introduction in 2018 led to stringent data privacy requirements, pushing a shift towards systems design rather than administration roles.
4.  The pandemic shifted lab management, multiplying research operations’ prominence and sustaining strategic systems design focus without reverting to previous structures.

Where does that leave us for the future then?

“In the next eight years then AI may well emerge as something that was seen as risk and ends up becoming a benefit to research.”

One of the exciting things about ResearchOps is how quickly it has developed. While AI is causing some trepidation for others, for those like Kate with an optimistic view of the industry then it will be just another potential bump that gets absorbed.
