There was a time when making software required a team.

You needed someone who understood the problem, someone who could design a solution, someone who could build it, someone who could test it, someone who could explain why it was worth using, and, occasionally, someone whose job was to stop everyone else from doing something catastrophically stupid.

It was an imperfect arrangement, naturally. Teams had meetings. Meetings had meetings about other meetings. Someone always wanted to change the shade of blue on a button that had already been approved three times.

### But there was a reason for all those people being in the same room: making a good product is not the same thing as making a product.

Then came Artificial Intelligence and suddenly, everyone could make software.

Designers could code. Developers could design. Marketing people could build applications. Someone who had spent the previous twenty years collecting vintage teapots could now launch a SaaS platform for the management of vintage teapot collections.

The kingdom of software had become a democratic republic, where everyone was a founder, everyone had a product, and everyone had a landing page with a gradient.

## Sweet Dreams (Are Made of This)

What we are witnessing is not simply the democratisation of software development. It is the transformation of almost everyone into a software content creator.

A little tool for a very specific problem. A dashboard for an industry that has never asked for a dashboard. An AI-powered assistant that does something that could probably be accomplished by a spreadsheet, a reasonably competent intern, or a small handwritten note.

There are applications for managing applications, tools for managing tools, platforms for connecting platforms, and AI agents whose primary responsibility appears to be helping other AI agents remember what they were supposed to be doing.

Every niche is going to be occupied. Every inconvenience is becoming a business opportunity. Every minor frustration is being converted into a subscription model.

The market is fragmenting into thousands of tiny kingdoms, each ruled by a founder with a logo, a Notion document, and a very optimistic pricing page.

### And because building has become easier, the number of things being built has exploded. But has the number of genuinely useful things increased at the same rate?

That is a rather different question.

Jorge Luis Borges imagined a universe in which an infinite library contained every possible combination of letters, every book that could ever be written, including the ones that made sense and the ones that consisted entirely of typographical accidents.\[1\]

It is difficult not to think of Borges when looking at today’s software ecosystem.

We are building our own Library of Babel. Except instead of books, we have micro-SaaS products, AI wrappers, productivity dashboards and niche applications. Somewhere in this enormous library, the perfect tool probably exists. Unfortunately, it is sitting between 400 nearly identical products, all claiming to have reinvented the future.

And the library, as Borges might have warned us, is not necessarily improved by the addition of another book.

## Who am I to disagree?

The individual is finally liberated from the constraints of the traditional organisation. No more waiting for engineering. No more negotiating with product management. No more asking for resources. No more dependency on other people’s expertise.

You can do everything yourself.

Slavoj Žižek once wrote:

> “We feel free because we lack the very language to articulate our unfreedom.”\[2\]

It is a rather uncomfortable observation to bring into the current AI conversation, because perhaps we are experiencing a similar contradiction.

We are told that we are becoming independent professionals, liberated from the organisational structures that previously limited our potential. But we are also becoming dependent on a completely different structure: platforms, algorithms, AI providers, subscription models, distribution channels and the relentless demands of personal visibility.

We have escaped the office only to discover that the office has followed us home, acquired a ring light and started demanding three LinkedIn posts a day + 2 videos and endless live streams.

This is not necessarily freedom. It might simply be a more sophisticated way of organising work.

Žižek discusses another useful formulation in _The Sublime Object of Ideology_, drawing on Peter Sloterdijk’s critique of cynical reason:

> “They know very well what they are doing, but still, they are doing it.”\[3\]

The original formulation belongs to Sloterdijk, but Žižek develops its implications for ideology, and it seems remarkably appropriate here.

We know that most products are unlikely to become successful. We know that the market is saturated. We know that visibility is increasingly expensive in terms of time and attention. We know that building something is only a fraction of the work required to make it viable.

And yet we keep building.

**Because the possibility of building has become so seductive that questioning whether something deserves to exist almost feels like an act of disloyalty to innovation itself.**

## I travel the world and the seven seas

Suddently you are the product manager, designer, engineer, researcher, QA specialist, copywriter, growth marketer, customer support representative, salesperson and, if things go particularly well, the person responsible for answering emails at two in the morning.

### **AI can help you perform many of these activities. It cannot automatically give you the professional judgement that comes from years of doing them.**

There is a difference between generating a user interface and understanding interaction design, or between producing code and understanding software architecture, or between generating a marketing strategy and understanding a market.

### There is a difference between making something work and knowing whether it should exist in the first place.

A machine can generate a plausible solution. It cannot guarantee that the person requesting it understands the problem, recognises the consequences or can evaluate the quality of the result.

This is where the old idea of professional collaboration becomes important again.

Teams were not simply expensive collections of people performing interchangeable tasks. They brought different forms of intelligence into the same process.

A designer might see a usability problem that an engineer cannot. An engineer might identify a structural problem that a product manager has overlooked. A researcher might discover that the entire problem being solved is imaginary.

This friction was not always inefficiency.

Sometimes it was quality control.

We seem to have mistaken the removal of organisational friction for the removal of professional complexity.

## (Hold your head up) movin’ on

There is an old story about a young apprentice who discovers that he can make magic happen without fully understanding the consequences. He gives a broom a command. The broom obeys. It fetches water. Then more water. Then more water. The apprentice has successfully automated a task.

![A minimalist black woodcut illustration of a startled apprentice wizard watching enchanted brooms carry buckets of water, spilling it across the floor.](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*cffHaIGhbhiW-qaCWgdwKA.png)

Unfortunately, he has not automated the judgement required to stop it.

Goethe wrote _The Sorcerer’s Apprentice_ in 1797. Disney later gave the story its famous cinematic interpretation in _Fantasia_.

It is difficult to imagine a more appropriate metaphor for certain aspects of AI-assisted production. We have acquired extraordinarily capable brooms.

They generate code, interfaces, documentation, images, business plans and marketing copy. They can build entire applications from instructions written by people who may have only a vague understanding of what they are asking for.

The broom works.

The problem is that nobody has necessarily thought about what happens when there are a thousand brooms, all building slightly different versions of the same thing.

## Keep your head up!

There is a moment where we will need to explain why our application is different from the other forty applications that do approximately the same thing.

This will grow a need to produce content about the product, content about the development of the product, content about the philosophy behind the product, and content about the content you are producing to promote the product.

Eventually, you may find yourself spending more time explaining that your software exists than actually improving it.

Guy Debord published _The Society of the Spectacle_ in 1967. His famous fourth thesis states:

> “The spectacle is not a collection of images; it is a social relation between people, mediated by images.”\[4\]

Debord was not writing about Social medialaunches or AI-powered SaaS products, obviously. He had other things to worry about.

But the observation is useful.

The product is increasingly becoming inseparable from its representation. The founder must be visible. The development process must be documented. The product must have a personality. Its creator must become a recognisable character in the story of its own existence.

The product launch becomes an event. The founder becomes a broadcaster. The development process becomes entertainment.

And the actual utility of the product is sometimes the least interesting part of the whole performance.

## 6\. Welcome to Private Television, 1987

Think back to the private television channels of the 1980s.

There were people selling carpets, mattresses, perfumes, jewellery, furniture, kitchen equipment and occasionally things whose purpose was never entirely clear.

They stood in front of a camera, holding their products with an enthusiasm that suggested either an extraordinary belief in humanity or a very urgent need to pay the electricity bill.

There was something wonderfully improvised about it.

I guess we all watched with a certain suspicion. Not necessarily because we thought everyone was a fraud, but because the whole operation felt a little makeshift. A little too eager. A little too convinced that whatever was being sold was the answer to a problem we had never realised we had.

Now look at the average software launch on social media. It looks very similar.

A founder in front of a camera. A carefully designed background. A polished landing page. A video explaining how their revolutionary tool will transform your workflow forever.

The lighting is better, maybe the typography too, and camera or microphone is considerably more expensive.

### But the underlying performance is remarkably similar.

The difference between a television salesman in 1987 and a founder launching an AI-powered productivity platform in 2026 is increasingly a matter of visual production values.

Why?

Because we are all studying the same successful products. We are all following the same launch strategies. We are all looking at the same examples of what works.

And then we wonder why the results begin to resemble one another.

We have democratised production without necessarily democratising originality.

## An unusual inversion of professional priorities.

In a traditional product organisation, marketing was supposed to help the product reach the right audience. The product had a purpose, and marketing communicated that purpose.

In the new individualist production model, the relationship can become reversed.

The need to produce content begins to determine what gets built.

What can be demonstrated in a thirty-second video? What looks impressive in a product launch? What can generate engagement? What is sufficiently novel to make people stop scrolling?

The product becomes partly an instrument for producing more content.

And this is how marketing quietly becomes more important than the thing being marketed.

## What comes next ?

The future of work should not be about eliminating everyone else from the room: it should be about using AI **to make collaboration more ambitious**, **rather than making isolation more productive.**

The point of technology should be to improve what we can do together, not simply to increase the number of things one exhausted individual can be expected to do alone.

### But if everyone is making things, everyone is also competing to be heard. And when everyone is competing to be heard, the loudest voice often wins over the most useful product.

We may end up with a generation of extraordinarily capable individuals spending most of their working lives promoting things they barely had time to understand, because the machine made the building part so easy that nobody remembered to ask whether the thing was worth building.

**We will have automated production and industrialised self-promotion.**

## Notes & References

**1\. Jorge Luis Borges — The Library of Babel (1941).**

A literary exploration of infinity, information, repetition and the limits of human interpretation. Particularly relevant to the paradox of an effectively unlimited supply of possible outputs.

[Read The Library of Babel](https://sites.evergreen.edu/politicalshakespeares/wp-content/uploads/sites/226/2015/12/Borges-The-Library-of-Babel.pdf)

**2\. Slavoj Žižek — Welcome to the Desert of the Real! (2002), Introduction: The Missing Ink, p. 2.**

The quotation about freedom and the language of unfreedom comes from Žižek’s discussion of ideology and the difficulty of articulating the conditions that structure our experience.

[Publication — Verso Books](https://www.versobooks.com/)

**3\. Slavoj Žižek — The Sublime Object of Ideology (1989), pp. 28–30.**

Žižek discusses cynical reason and Peter Sloterdijk’s formulation of ideological participation. The distinction in attribution matters: the familiar formula originates with Sloterdijk, while Žižek develops its ideological implications.

[The Sublime Object of Ideology — text reference](https://www.thetedkarchive.com/library/slavoj-zizek-the-sublime-object-of-ideology)

**4\. Guy Debord — The Society of the Spectacle (1967), Thesis 4.**

Debord’s analysis of the spectacle as a social relationship mediated by images provides the conceptual foundation for the argument about software founders becoming performers and products becoming promotional narratives.

[Read The Society of the Spectacle](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/debord/society.htm)

**5\. Walter Benjamin — The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction (1936).**

Benjamin examines how mechanical reproduction transforms the status, experience and authority of artworks. The essay offers a useful historical parallel for thinking about AI-generated production and the changing relationship between technical reproduction and cultural value.

[Read the essay](https://www.marxists.org/reference/subject/philosophy/works/ge/benjamin.htm)

**6\. Johann Wolfgang von Goethe — The Sorcerer’s Apprentice (1797).**

The poem explores the consequences of using powers without understanding how to control them. Its best-known popular adaptation is the animated sequence in Disney’s _Fantasia_ (1940).

[Read the poem — Poetry Foundation](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/poems/45937/the-sorcerers-apprentice)
