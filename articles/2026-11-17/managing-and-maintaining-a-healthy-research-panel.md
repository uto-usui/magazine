_This is part four of a_ [_four part series_](https://medium.com/@thomas.n.anne/the-realities-of-participant-panels-c41463fc9a14?sharedUserId=thomas.n.anne) _on panel management._

Getting a panel started isn’t easy, so if you’ve successfully set one up, congratulations and respect are in order. But keeping it healthy over time is a different kind of challenge.

Panels aren’t Twinkies™ or forever chemicals. They are subject to entropy, and they require energy to keep functioning. The enthusiasm and collaboration that fueled the launch can fade as the reality of ongoing maintenance sets in.

This article looks at how practitioners keep their panels healthy without losing momentum: keeping participants engaged without burning them out, handling participant data responsibly, improving representation, and managing the ongoing work sustainably.

### Keep Participants Engaged

Sustaining participant engagement means treating the panel not as a static database, but as an ongoing relationship that requires “care and feeding.” Interviewees stressed that when participants feel their time is being wasted or their voices aren’t being heard, they can quickly disengage or stop responding.

Practitioners used several tactics to maintain engagement.

### Humanize the Panel to Build Rapport

Rachel Wigen-Toccalino, a Staff UXR Program Manager, worked with a team in the healthcare space, where showing care for participants was paramount. To build rapport from the start, Rachel’s team provided a high-touch onboarding experience that included an orientation and a clear schedule of weekly communications.

Ted Kendall, a Market Researcher across Consumer Packaged Goods, packaging, and product development, kept engagement high by putting a human face on the panel. His wife served as the dedicated “face” of the program, handling communications and signing off with her name. The connection became strong enough that participants would email her to let her know when they were going on vacation and couldn’t take a survey.

> “Part of the success of the panel was that personal rapport that they had. It was a real person. It wasn’t just Kraft. \[The participants\] felt important, and they felt like their feedback was being listened to,” said Kendall.

Thriving panels put a face to the name. According to User Interviews internal data, among teams that met its success threshold of 100+ sessions, 90.3% had set up a custom sender profile, compared with 76.5% of other teams, 56.0% used a logo, compared with 23.5%, and 76.1% used a custom theme, compared with 64.7%.

### Close the Loop Through Sharebacks and Instant Gratification

Participants want to know that their feedback matters. Practitioners found ways to show panelists the impact of their participation, both large and small. Tanya Branitska, a Market Researcher in the software space, uses polls that display results immediately, providing what she calls “instant gratification”. Arya Guinney, a Consumer Insights Researcher for a national fast food chain, uses larger “sharebacks” as a core engagement tactic, showing participants how their input has influenced the menu and reinforcing their connection to the brand.

### Provide Non-Research Value and Relevant Content

To prevent panel fatigue, some managers interact with panelists outside of direct research requests to keep the relationship warm. Joanne Van Vranken, a Research Operations Manager in the accounting software industry, works with specialised tradespeople and realized that some segments of her panel weren’t called upon for research very often. She began sending quarterly newsletters with fun, non-business polls simply to maintain the connection and say, “We haven’t forgotten that you’re here!”

### Align Incentives with What Participants Value

While monetary incentives are common, practitioners also described rewards that align with the participants’ professional goals, interests, or brand loyalty. Prama Ayalasomayajula, a UX Researcher at a B2B CRM company, found that giving her panel of B2B advertising professionals direct access to the people building the product could be more motivating than monetary compensation.

For her panel of busy restaurant owners, Marta Paz, a User Researcher in the restaurant software industry, taps into their desire to stay ahead of what’s coming next.

“What they want is to know what is new with us, so that they are part of the group that is gonna change things in the company. That they will be able to test first all the main features that come out \[is their main incentive\].”

### Avoid Participant Burnout

Keeping panelists active is the lifeblood of a healthy panel. Practitioners described several ways to prevent burnout, from carefully managing contact frequency to reducing the burden of individual studies.

### Enforce Cooldown Periods and Rotations

Jess Ivins, a Senior UX Researcher who runs a B2B panel, uses her panel management software’s built-in tools to prevent repeatedly contacting the same participants. “That way we avoid that problem of asking the same people over and over again with an automatic ninety-day cooldown.”

Avinash Mangar, a Research Project Manager in the entertainment industry, worked with a team that surveyed fans as often as two to four times a week about programming. To manage that frequency, they rotated who received each invitation. “We’re all constantly rotating and refreshing who gets contacted.”

### Keep Surveys Short and Eliminate Tedious Question Types

Managing frequency is only part of the equation. Practitioners also looked for ways to reduce the burden of each individual study.

Arun Upadhyay, a Research Ops Specialist in the market research industry, sets a 20-minute limit on online interviews for his government research panel. “We ensure that the length of any interview is twenty minutes online. Otherwise it is way boring for them.”

Market Researcher Ted Kendall found that certain question formats contributed to participant drop-off, so he eliminated grid questions. “We got a ten percent drop-off with the grid.”

### Utilize Progressive Profiling to Avoid Repetition

Practitioners noted that repeatedly asking participants for the same demographic or firmographic information can create unnecessary friction. Katie Encabo, a Client Success Manager who worked with a panel of rural service providers, found that appending the same questions to every survey added to the burden. She recommends using progressive profiling to retain profile information so “you don’t have to refill that information every time.”

### Use Highly Targeted Invitations to Ensure Relevance

Even a short survey can become frustrating when it isn’t relevant to the person receiving it. Karen Bills, a Research Ops Specialist across marketplaces and social media platforms, heard complaints from participants who were repeatedly invited to studies that didn’t fit their work: “I keep getting these surveys about campaigns, and I’m a marketer, but I’m not related to campaigns.”

Her takeaway was simple: understand your audience and “make sure you’re targeting the invites accordingly.”

### Avoid Consent Drift with Careful Data Handling

While participant burnout risks losing participant engagement over time, consent violations and poor data handling can put the panel itself at risk.

Interviewees cautioned against the ‘consent drift’, where changes, merges, or transfers between systems can result in people being added to outreach they didn’t consent to receive. Over time, that can lead to poor data hygiene, accidental over-contacting, or consent violations.

To reduce that risk, practitioners recommend carefully managing data merges and, where appropriate, using integrations or automations to carry consent and opt-out preferences across systems.

### Purge Non-consenting Users from Data Merges

Consent problems can emerge inadvertently when new data sources are connected to a panel. Jess Ivins, a Senior UX Researcher in enterprise software, experienced this when her data team synced its Snowflake(™) database directly to the panel tool, RallyCRM. The integration risked pulling thousands of users into the panel without the required panel consent. Jess now purges non-consenting users from the platform to maintain panel integrity.

### Keep Consent Updated by Inheriting Opt-out Rules

Compliance isn’t just about the initial sign-up. It requires maintaining accurate consent and preference records over time, including when participants opt out or request deletion.

Market Researcher Tanya Branitska suggests establishing an automated opt-out inheritance workflow, where appropriate. “Set up an automated link between the panel and your native software to have that \[opt-out\] process automated.”

**Case Study:** Arya Guinney, a Consumer Insights Researcher at a national restaurant chain, recognized that her company had separate marketing and research lists, and an opt-out in one system wasn’t automatically reflected in another. Guinney addressed this through clear communication, ensuring participants understood exactly what they were signing up for and how different types of communication were treated separately.

The goal was not only to meet legal requirements, but also to make participants’ choices clear, including how they could withdraw their consent. “I want to be clear from a legal perspective, but also from a user experience perspective that these are separate things and you can rescind your opt-in at any time.”

### Increase and Maintain Healthy Representation

A panel that skews toward your easiest, most enthusiastic respondents will quietly tell you a distorted story. The people who respond most readily may not reflect the full range of people you need to hear from, and that imbalance can grow over time.

Practitioners we spoke with pointed to two habits for maintaining representation over time: treating multicultural audiences as distinct groups rather than monoliths, and actively replenishing the segments that become underrepresented.

### Treat Multicultural Audiences as Distinct Groups, Not Monoliths

Avinash Mangar emphasized the importance of recognizing differences within ethnic and regional populations rather than treating them as homogeneous groups. “You can’t treat certain ethnic or regional groups like monoliths, expecting their behaviors and preferences to be the same.” In international research, Mangar also adapts language and localization to better engage different audiences.

### Oversample or Continuously Recruit Underrepresented Populations

The diversity you recruit for at launch doesn’t stay put. Smaller segments can become underrepresented as participants leave the panel, while the people who remain may change over time. A participant’s role, life stage, or relationship with your product can shift enough that they no longer represent the segment you originally recruited them for.

Karen Bills, a Research Ops Specialist across marketplaces and social media platforms, runs ongoing replenishment campaigns that deliberately recruit for populations that have become harder to reach or underrepresented in the panel. As one example, someone who was a “career starter” two years ago may no longer fit that segment.

> “Two years later, they’re not the same person anymore. And we need to backfill to make sure we still have that audience represented.”

### Improve Efficiency with Automation

Interviewees described operational efficiency as key to turning panel management from a reactive administrative burden into a more scalable resource. They focused on reducing time to recruit, automating repetitive administrative tasks, and moving away from scattered, manual systems.

Panel management systems can reduce some of the back-and-forth involved in moving participants into studies. Practitioners use both built-in workflows and custom automations to streamline recruitment, study administration, and other repetitive tasks.

According to User Interviews internal data, highly effective teams defined as those running more than 100 sessions were more likely to connect Zoom (54.5% vs 45.5%) and Microsoft (26.9% vs 17.8%), While this doesn’t establish causation, it suggests that more productive teams are more likely to integrate tools into their workflows.

### Make it Easy for Stakeholders to Say “Yes” to Your Campaign

Orrin Ward, UX & ResearchOps Lead in FinTech, launched an initiative called “Project Easy Peasy” to make it easier for B2B account managers to review participant contact requests. To reduce the burden of approval, Ward built an automation that allowed account managers to approve or reject requests with a simple button press.

### Leverage Long-term Consent Contracts for Faster Scheduling

To streamline the booking process and lower the barrier for panelists, one ResearchOps Lead in a biosciences organization, replaced per-session consent forms with a one-year master consent agreement administered through DocuSign.

### Use Database Integrations to Reduce Manual Work

Integrating internal data sources with panel tools can reduce manual data management. Agustina Di Clemente, Research Operations Specialist for B2B Travel Management Software, partnered with her data team to integrate Snowflake directly into Great Question. The integration automatically synced and updated panelist attributes, eliminating the need to manually pull demographic lists from another tool.

### Establish Self-Serve Models

Efficiency isn’t just about technology; it’s also about how teams access and work with the panel. Several practitioners described using self-service workflows to distribute some of the operational work. Jen Cardey, a Research Operations Manager, previously launched “Research Lite” at TripAdvisor, a self-service program that allowed non-researchers, including PMs and designers, to utilize the panel independently, reducing reliance on the Research Ops team for every request.

### Measure Success

When it comes to evaluating the success of your panel, activity is easy to measure: sessions run, emails sent, participants recruited. But activity is not the same as success.

A panel is successful when it solves the problem it was built to solve, and that problem is different from team to team. Before choosing a metric, go back to the reason you built the panel in the first place.

Were you trying to shorten time to recruitment? Improve the quality of your insights? Democratize who in the organization could run research? Reduce your reliance on expensive external recruiters?

A growing contact count can feel like progress even if half those contacts have gone cold. Choosing the right metric helps distinguish between a panel that is genuinely working and one that only looks busy.

Below, we’ve compiled a set of metrics to consider and, where available, benchmarks that can provide context for what “healthy” looks like.

![Table of six ways to measure panel success, each with a reason, what to track, and a benchmark. Time to data: approval to sessions filled. Metrics can’t tell you exactly what to do next, but they give you a place to start forming hypotheses and looking for the story underneath. Choose the one or two measures that map most directly to why your panel exists, then use them consistently to understand where the panel is working and where it needs attention.

### When to Shut Down a Panel

Even after employing the tactics above, panels can still fail due to supply, demand, organizational, or strategic factors. Knowing when the underlying conditions have changed is just as important as knowing how to keep a panel going.

### Lack of Internal Buy-In and Executive Pushback

A major factor in shutting down or failing to launch a panel is lack of support from leadership or key internal teams. Market Researcher Ted Kendall described an initiative at a previous company that was killed simply because “the VP of marketing didn’t like panels, and she was very strong in her opinions.”

### Low Engagement and Wasted Effort

When the effort to maintain a panel consistently outweighs the value it produces, it might be time to reconsider it. Research Operations Manager Joanne Van Vranken shared that her panel was disbanded after two years of operation. “We’re putting all this effort into trying to build this panel, and it really hasn’t become what we had hoped it would become.” She attributed part of this to poor internal visibility, noting, “We never were actually were able to communicate to the firm as a whole what the panel was actually for.”

### Shifting Priorities and Lack of Dedicated Time

When researchers must balance panel management with other pressing priorities, the panel can suffer. Weiyan Chee, a Research Operations Lead in the healthcare industry, noted that her panel eventually “wittled away” because “all these different priorities took up most of my time”. Mirjam De Klepper, a Senior UX Researcher for a B2B software company, experienced something similar when the broader project changed direction and the panel eventually died down.

### Company Layoffs and Strategic Pivots

Abrupt organizational changes, such as layoffs or shifting strategies, can bring even a healthy panel to an unexpected end. Senior Research Operations Specialist Emily Wever had a panel that ended overnight when “the research team was eliminated at the company,” leaving only “maybe eighteen hours between the email and the layoff the next morning”.

### Planned Ephemerality

Not every panel is meant to last forever. Rachel Wigen-Toccalino, a Staff UXR Program Manager in the healthcare industry, structured her panels to wrap up after a defined period, noting that her team hasn’t “extended it beyond six months”.

### The panel is never finished

Deciding whether to build or buy, making the case for the investment, setting the panel up, and keeping it alive are not four separate projects. They’re parts of one continuous commitment to the people who help shape your product.

So if you’re staring down the maintenance phase and feeling the momentum slip, that’s not necessarily a sign you’ve failed. It’s the normal physics of a panel. Panels are subject to entropy. Keeping one healthy is the price of having a standing bench of real users ready to answer your hardest questions when you need them.

Very few research assets pay off like that. Almost none of them run themselves.
