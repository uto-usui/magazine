Welcome to Source Material, a weekly series where the designers and creatives shaping culture share the references that shaped them. Our next guest is [Zach Lieberman](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CaF0t8QrQjE), a New York–based artist and educator who co-founded the [School for Poetic Computation](https://sfpc.study/), helped develop [openFrameworks](https://openframeworks.cc/), and teaches at [MIT Media Lab](https://www.media.mit.edu/).

After the shoot, we caught up with Zach about his creative process:

**What were you making when you realized, “This could be my job”?**

I studied fine art, drawing, and printmaking, and I struggled when I started using a computer because it felt less expressive than the pencils and paint brushes I was used to. But then I made a simple coding experiment where you draw a line, and it uncurls itself. It was so alive and organic, and I felt confident after that point that I could spend my career asking weird questions like that.

**What’s the first thing you do when you sit down to work?**

I often sketch on paper and map out some ideas I want to try. I also open up yesterday’s project and run it a few times to think about how I can change something old to make it something new.

**What’s a design object you’d take to your grave?**

The original Mac computers, like [Mac SE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macintosh_SE)—I still remember how the mouse felt coming from an MS-DOS machine with green type. The idea that you could click anywhere on the screen opened up worlds for me. I would sit at my neighbor’s house and use the paint can tool in MacPaint and just click and click and click.

**Whose work makes you jealous?**

I’m jealous of a lot of people in my field, but mostly I’m jealous of artists who lived at different moments and got to explore a new medium for the first time. [Lillian Schwartz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lillian_Schwartz) at Bell Labs working with early computer animation, [Myron Krueger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myron_W._Krueger) doing the first body-based interaction, etc. It must have felt wild to be at the start of something.

**What current design trend are you tired of seeing?**

I’m very tired of seeing AI use which stops at the output—I think AI can be a useful tool for conversation, but it’s becoming maddening to see posters and images in delis that are clearly just outputs from AI.

**What rule do you break the most?**

Don’t repeat yourself! I find repeating yourself to be the most interesting thing you can do. You see old ideas with new eyes.

**What song do you put on to get out of a creative rut?**

Anything by [Hiroshi Yoshimura](https://open.spotify.com/artist/1DGpHnPOpMYY780hcQHmPB).

**What’s a piece of feedback you resisted, and later realized was right?**

Write more. I find writing hard, but every time I do, I understand myself better. Writing (like coding) is a form of thinking.

**What’s a part of your process that most people don’t know about?**

I really like to break things! I often think my job is to turn the volume knob up to 11 and break a piece of code in order to see what’s possible.

**What’s the last thing you came across that made you want to go make something?**

I found this book called “Learning by Heart” by [Sister Corita Kent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corita_Kent), who did all these wild typographic posters. It’s got one of the worst covers, but the inside is all about the joy of making art. It’s lowercase “art,” art as a verb, not uppercase “Art,” art as something you need to worry about or justify. I love the feeling I get reading that book, and it made me excited to experiment and play. I often share her rules with my students, but this was the first time I’ve gotten deeper into her writings.
