Summary:  Usability testing involves watching people use a design to uncover problems and opportunities. Anyone on a product team can learn this.

[Usability](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-101-introduction-to-usability/) testing (sometimes called **user testing**) is a popular UX-research method.

> **Usability testing** is a research method in which researchers observe people attempting a realistic task with one or more user interfaces, to identify usability problems and opportunities for improvement.

## Why Run a Usability Test?

The goals of usability testing vary by study, but they usually include:

-   **Identifying problems** in the design of the product or service
-   **Uncovering opportunities** to improve
-   **Learning about the target user’s** behavior and preferences

![Why Usability Test? Three goals: uncover problems in the design, discover opportunities to improve the design, and learn about users’ behavior and preferences.](https://media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2026/11/26/usability-tests-uncover-problems-opportunities-and-needs_0WhM7BW.png)

_Usability testing helps us to uncover problems, discover opportunities, and learn about users._

Why do we need to do usability testing? [Won’t a good professional UX designer know](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/the-myth-of-the-genius-designer/) how to design a great user interface? Even the best UX designers (or the best AI-based design tool) can’t design a perfect user experience without [**iterative design**](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/parallel-and-iterative-design/) **driven by observations of real users** interacting with the design.

That’s because a design pattern that works in one interface can confuse people in another, depending on what surrounds it and what users are trying to do. Users also bring [mental models](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mental-models/) shaped by the other products they’ve used, and a design that doesn’t match those expectations can trip them up. With so many factors at play, **no designer (or AI) can reliably predict how people will interact with a design without observing them**.

## Elements of Usability Testing

There are many different types of usability testing, but the core elements in most usability tests are **the facilitator, the tasks, a design, and the participant**.

![What each element is: the facilitator guides the session without influencing; tasks are realistic activities the user might do in real life; the participant is a realistic user of the design being tested; and the design is the interface or prototype being tested.](https://media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2026/11/26/4-core-elements-of-usability-testing.png)

_Most usability tests involve four core elements: a facilitator, tasks, a design, and a participant._

The facilitator administers tasks to the participant. As the participant performs these tasks, the facilitator observes the participant’s behavior and listens for feedback. The facilitator may also ask followup questions to elicit detail from the participant.

![The facilitator gives tasks to the participant and then observes and interviews the participant while they attempt them.](https://media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2026/11/26/facilitators-give-tasks-then-watch-and-listen.png)

_In a usability test, the facilitator gives instructions and task scenarios to the participant. The participant provides behavioral and verbal feedback about the interface while they perform those tasks._

### Facilitator

The **facilitator** [guides the participant](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-checklist/) through the test process. They give instructions, answer the participant’s questions, and ask followup questions.

The facilitator works to ensure that the test results in high-quality, valid data, without [accidentally influencing the participant’s behavior](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/leading-questions/). Achieving this balance is difficult and requires training.

(In one form of remote usability testing, called [remote unmoderated testing](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/unmoderated-usability-testing/), an application may perform some of the facilitator’s roles.)

### Tasks

The **tasks** in a usability test are realistic activities that the participant might perform in real life. They can be very specific or very open-ended, [depending on the research questions](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-research-goals-to-scenarios/) and the type of usability testing.

Examples of tasks from real usability studies:

> _Your printer is showing “Error 5200”. Figure out how to clear it._

> _You're considering opening a new credit card with Wells Fargo. Please visit wellsfargo.com and decide which credit card you might want to open, if any._

> _You’ve been told you need to speak to Tyler Smith from the Project Management department. Use the intranet to find out where they are located. Tell the researcher your answer._

[Task wording](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/better-usability-tasks/) is very important in usability testing. Small errors in the phrasing of a task can cause the participant to misunderstand what they’re asked to do or can influence how participants perform the task (a psychological phenomenon called [priming](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/priming/)).

Task instructions can be delivered to the participant verbally (the facilitator might read them) or can be handed to a participant written down (for example on a piece of paper, or through a chat interface in remote studies). We often ask participants to read the task instructions out loud. This helps ensure that the participant reads the instructions completely, and helps the facilitator and observers with their notetaking, because they always know which task the user is performing.

### Participant

The **participant** should be a [realistic user](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/recruiting-test-participants-for-usability-studies/) of the product or service being studied: either a current user or someone with similar needs or background.

Whenever possible, include participants with disabilities, such as people who use screen readers or other assistive technologies. They’ll reveal problems that other participants won’t encounter. (See [How to Conduct Usability Studies for Accessibility](https://www.nngroup.com/reports/how-to-conduct-usability-studies-accessibility/) for practical guidance.)

Participants are often asked to [**think out loud**](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/thinking-aloud-the-1-usability-tool/) during usability testing (called the “think-aloud method”). The facilitator might ask the participants to narrate their actions and thoughts as they perform tasks. The goal of this approach is to understand participants’ behaviors, goals, thoughts, and motivations.

![](https://media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2019/11/11/usabilitytesting101-29.png)

_In this in-person usability-test session, the participant uses a special testing laptop, which is running screen-recording software. The laptop has a webcam to capture the participant’s facial expressions and is connected to an external monitor for the facilitator. The facilitator listens to his feedback, provides tasks, and takes notes._

## Types of Usability Testing

### Qualitative vs. Quantitative

Usability testing can be either [qualitative or quantitative](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/quant-vs-qual/).

> **Qualitative usability testing** focuses on collecting insights, findings, and anecdotes about how people use the product or service. Qualitative usability testing is best for discovering problems in the user experience. This form of usability testing is more common than quantitative usability testing.

> **Quantitative usability testing** focuses on collecting metrics that describe the user experience. Two of the metrics most commonly collected in quantitative usability testing are [task success](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/success-rate-the-simplest-usability-metric/) and time on task. Quantitative usability testing is often used for measuring usability, comparing designs, and tracking usability improvements over time.

The number of participants needed for a usability test varies depending on the type of study. For a typical **qualitative usability study** of a single user group, we recommend [**using five participants**](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/why-you-only-need-to-test-with-5-users/) to uncover most of the common problems in the product.

### Remote vs. In-Person Testing

Remote usability tests are popular because they often require less time and money than in-person studies. There are two types of remote usability testing: [moderated and unmoderated](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/remote-usability-tests/) (see our [Remote Usability Testing: Study Guide](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/remote-usability-testing-study-guide/)).

> [**Remote moderated**](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/moderated-remote-usability-test/) usability tests work very similarly to in-person studies. The facilitator interacts with the participant and asks them to perform tasks. However, the facilitator and participant are in different physical locations. Usually, moderated tests are performed using video-conferencing software.

> [**Remote unmoderated**](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/unmoderated-usability-testing/) usability tests do not have the same facilitator–participant interaction as in-person or moderated tests. The person running the study uses a dedicated [online remote-testing tool](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/unmoderated-user-testing-tools/) to set up written tasks for the participant. Then, the participant completes those tasks alone, on their own time. The testing tool delivers the task instructions and any followup questions. After the participant completes the test, the researcher receives a recording of the session, along with metrics like task success.

![Information flow in a remote unmoderated test: the researcher writes tasks and the participant performs them on the testing platform; the platform's video recording captures the participant, and the researcher watches it.](https://media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2026/11/26/remote-unmoderated-flow-of-information.png)

_In remote unmoderated testing, the researcher and participant never interact directly; the testing platform takes on the facilitator’s role._

### Usability Testing and AI

AI can save time on some parts of a usability study. **AI transcription** turns session recordings into searchable transcripts, with timestamps that link each line to that moment in the video. This makes it faster to find key moments and assemble **highlight reels**: short clips that show stakeholders what participants experienced.

AI can help with speeding up analysis and reporting by helping you identify issues, find quotes, and clip recordings. But they can make mistakes in data analysis, so researchers need to verify AI-generated interpretation against the original data. Finally, some newer research tools offer [AI moderators](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ai-interviewers/). They work like remote unmoderated testing: you prepare the test in advance, and an AI moderator runs it for you. It can guide participants through the interaction and ask followup questions, but current tools have limitations. Some cannot fully observe participants’ interactions with the interface and they may struggle to ask appropriate questions. For now, they are best suited for relatively simple studies. (Learn more about using AI in usability testing in our [Accelerating Research with AI](https://www.nngroup.com/courses/research-with-ai/) live online course.)

## Cost of Usability Testing

Simple, [“discount” usability](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/discount-usability-20-years/) studies can be inexpensive, though you usually must provide an incentive to participants. Sessions can take place remotely over a video call or in a conference room, and the simplest study can take as little as 3 days of your time (assuming that you have already learned how to do it, and you have access to participants):

-   Day 1: Plan the study
-   Day 2: Test with 5 users
-   Day 3: Analyze the findings and convert them into redesign recommendations for the next iteration

AI tools can save some of that time (for example, by drafting tasks or transcribing sessions), but someone on the team still needs to watch the sessions and analyze what participants did. 

Additionally, as AI tools make it possible to build and change interfaces more quickly, teams may need to conduct usability testing more frequently to ensure that design decisions remain grounded in real user behavior.

Usability studies can become more expensive (i.e., can cost thousands of dollars) if you choose to recruit larger sample sizes or use more advanced methods. Things that add cost include:

-   [Competitive testing](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/competitive-usability-evaluations/) of multiple designs
-   [International testing](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/international-usability-testing/) in multiple countries
-   Testing with multiple user groups (or [personas](https://www.nngroup.com/courses/personas/))
-   Quantitative studies (where you need a larger sample size)
-   Use of fancy equipment like [eyetrackers](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/eyetracking-setup/)
-   A dedicated usability lab to [allow others to observe](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/observer-guidelines/) in person (remote sessions let others observe from anywhere)

The [return on investment](https://www.nngroup.com/courses/measuring-ux/) (ROI) for advanced studies can still be high, though usually not as high as that for simple studies.
