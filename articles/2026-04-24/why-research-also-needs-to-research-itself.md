We have been living in a paradox that is no longer new. Never have we had so much access to information and, at the same time, never have we been so exposed to misinformation. Biased reports, dashboards that impress them more by volume than by clarity, statistics without context or source. All this forces us to revisit a fundamental question: how can we trust the data we use to make decisions?

This concern led me to revisit concepts of Metadesign, especially through the reflections of Caio Vassão, and to deepen my study of meta-research.

In simple terms, metadesign can be understood as the “design of design,” that is, a reflective process dedicated to improving the methods, tools, and conditions that allow design to take place. More than designing objects or services, it invites us to reflect on how we design systems, processes, and ecosystems of knowledge, especially in contexts marked by complexity, ambiguity, and constant change.

#### Meta-research – research about research

Like metadesign, meta-research is, in essence, research about research itself. It is a field dedicated to examining, critiquing, and improving the practices, processes, and policies that sustain the production of knowledge: how it is conceived, conducted, communicated, evaluated, replicated, and rewarded.

According to [Stanford METRICS](https://metrics.stanford.edu/) and the [Meta-Research community at NYU Langone](https://med.nyu.edu/research/meta-research/), meta-research operates across different dimensions of the scientific process. It analyzes methods, investigates how results are reported, discusses reproducibility, proposes alternatives to traditional peer review, and examines incentive systems that shape research behavior. In other words, science has only advanced because it created formal rituals to review its own practice.

Although these debates are relatively consolidated in the scientific field, they are still little explored in a systematic way in the corporate world, especially in market research, product research, user experience research, and consumer behavior studies.

That is what I mean.

#### When research stops being an investigative tool

Since 2018, I have been working primarily with qualitative research for companies across different industries and levels of maturity. In this experience, I have noticed a persistent pattern: a crystallization of methods. What I mean by this is that instead of starting from the problem to choose the most appropriate approach, many organizations start from an already established method and shape the problem around it.

The justification that “we’ve always done it this way” or “this model already works” usually relies on historical efficiency, especially in more traditional environments. The side effect is subtle but, in my view, highly relevant: research stops being an investigative tool and begins to operate as a standardized protocol, one that is not very sensitive to the nuances of context.

In practice, this translates into constant methodological conflicts. There have been cases in which I suggested leaner qualitative approaches capable of generating much deeper insights with greater agility, whether in satisfaction research, user experience studies, trend research, or consumer behavior studies. Even so, the recurring demand was for numbers, large quantitative datasets, and extensive dashboards, even when these visualizations revealed little about what people were actually experiencing, feeling, or facing in their journeys and in their relationships with products and services. The result is a type of analysis that prioritizes the description of quantitative data, but not necessarily a deeper understanding of the experiences that produce them.

In this context, resistance also manifested itself in the limited appreciation for the structural work that sustains research itself, such as the curation and continuous organization of data repositories. Even when a study is well planned and structured, the process during research does not necessarily unfold in a linear sequence across data collection, analysis, and final delivery. As fieldwork progresses, hypotheses shift, hidden data emerge, and new questions arise throughout the research process.

Data organization follows this movement and, for this reason, is not merely an operational step but a constitutive part of the research itself. Yet these activities, which are fundamental for ensuring consistency, speed, and reliability, are often perceived as costs or delays, when in practice they are precisely what makes it possible to produce analyses capable of revealing dimensions of experience that numbers alone cannot show.

#### Questioning research approaches

These conflicts are rarely only technical. Most of the time, they are cultural. Many organizations present themselves as innovative when talking about products, technologies, or business models, but this openness does not always extend to their investigative methods. Revisiting approaches, questioning processes, or proposing new ways of conducting research can be less perceived as methodological maturity and more as operational risk. The result is an environment in which research continues to be produced but is rarely reflected upon in its own practices.

It is at this point that meta-research brings an important provocation to the corporate world. Instead of treating methods as fixed protocols, it invites us to view the production of knowledge itself as something open to review, learning, and evolution. In many companies, however, methodologies and conclusions end up being treated as stabilized truths, even when context, users, and products themselves continue to change.

Therefore, the issue is not only applying research methods but creating conditions for those methods to evolve as well. After all, the presence of specialists or dedicated research teams does not, by itself, guarantee that organizational decisions are truly guided by evidence.

The question is not merely applying the scientific model within organizations but recognizing that decisions are not guided only by evidence. Knowledge and power coexist in permanent tension in these environments, influencing which data gain space in discussions and which ultimately get ignored.

In this sense, I understand meta-research as a living process that is not limited to the academic environment. It can and should inspire more effective organizational practices, especially in the high-speed and highly complex scenario we live in today.

#### The critical compass

With the accelerated advancement of artificial intelligence, this reflection becomes even more urgent. If reviewing research once required months or years, today AI systems can analyze large volumes of data, comparing studies, and identifying patterns rapidly. Still, no technology replaces the critical human perspective. Artificial intelligence can accelerate the process, but the responsibility and insight required to make improvements remain human — they remain ours.

For this reason, I believe meta-research acts as a kind of critical compass. By revisiting how we conduct research, how we evaluate results, and how we correct failures, meta-research establishes parameters so that technological acceleration does not become uncritical automation.

In other words, if artificial intelligence expands speed, meta-research sustains direction.

As a user experience researcher, I have always advocated for the implementation of lessons-learned rituals at the end of every project. Despite having experienced resistance to this practice — often postponed in favor of agendas considered more urgent, I defend this ritual as a structuring stage of research projects in corporate environments, because these moments go beyond reviewing results. When formalized, they become concrete mechanisms of quality and governance: they record methodological decisions made throughout the process, make explicit the criteria adopted, document identified biases, and consolidate limitations and recommendations for future studies.

By making the research journey visible, we create a legitimate space for methodological debate and for the accumulation of organizational knowledge. It is about replacing the automatic repetition of “we’ve always done it this way” with a reflective practice that recognizes method as something alive, capable of revision and continuous improvement.

It is this ongoing exercise of critical reflection that strengthens the reliability of data, sustains the cohesion of research teams, and contributes to more responsible, conscious, and sustainable organizational decisions.

If I began this article by asking how we can trust the data we use to make decisions, perhaps the answer lies less in the number of studies produced and more in how we organize, revisit, and integrate the knowledge that already exists.

As Jake Burghardt suggests in Stop Wasting Research, the real risk is not only producing weak data, but wasting it due to inadequate preparation, lack of motivation among stakeholders, and weak integration with product and organizational processes. In a world where data is abundant, but trust is scant, trust requires method, memory, and continuous revision.

And the question changes: are we merely producing research, or are we building organizational knowledge capable of evolving over time?
