Matthew Santone on the changing face of design

![A pixelated portrait of a man’s face is knitted on fabric, attached to a white yarn spiraling outward on a red background.](https://microsoft.design/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Hero-CC-3.png)

For most of my career, craft was something I hid behind.

I don’t mean that cynically. Craft is hard work, and getting great at it takes years. But the presence of that craft did something I didn’t notice until it was gone. It answered the question before anyone could ask it.

_Does this person know what they’re doing?_

The pixels were pushed, the type was tight. The artifact spoke so I didn’t have to.

Then the cost of all that craft fell.

I can describe an interface and watch it appear. I can build an app between two meetings. The execution, the part I hid behind, is now nearly free. And standing in the middle of all this capability, I keep arriving at the same uncomfortable place. The artifact can’t speak for me anymore, because making it is no longer the hard part. That leaves one question. 

_Do I actually have anything to say?_

This is the naked designer. Not exposed by AI, but by what it made cheap. A thread pulled. When making something is no longer evidence you meant it, there’s nowhere left to hide.

![A single red string or rope forms a gentle curve against a plain white background.](https://microsoft.design/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/thread-2-1.png)

## What craft had become

At first I didn’t understand. The thing I was good at turned out to be reducible. The magic was just the parts I couldn’t see. Underneath, a question. If the craft was never rare, was I? The easy thing to do is blame the tool. But the tool didn’t take anything away. It pulled that thread.

That’s what craft had quietly become. Not just a skill, a garment. I’m happiest when the artifact carries the meeting and I get to be quiet. For years I told myself that was humility. But there’s a version of that posture that isn’t humility at all. It’s hiding. If the work always speaks, you never find out whether you can.

And now the work itself is losing its credibility. Not because it isn’t good, but because anyone can make a good-enough thing in an afternoon. When finish was expensive, it was a signal. Now the polish is free, and a free signal is no signal at all. Pull that thread and the whole garment comes apart, and the room turns to you, waiting for you to say something.

Here’s the trap. You go to cover before you ever feel the exposure. The cover is fluency, a garment only you can see. You reach for AI, take its answer even if it’s wrong, and feel more sure of yourself. Psychologists call it cognitive surrender. You can stand there fully exposed, having said nothing, and feel like you nailed it.

From there the flight is easy, and it runs one direction: make more. I know, because it’s the advice I used to give. Be the conductor. Let judgment sort the signal from the noise. It sounds like wisdom until you notice its permission to never stop and ask whether you had anything to say.

You were good before. Be good a hundred times faster. Produce and produce, and never ask the question, while the value of all that output quietly falls. This is the slop. Not bad work made by a machine, but the exhaust of people running from a question they couldn’t sit with. The way out was never more. It was inward. It was the thing you actually had to say.

## Twenty years with Poe

I’ve never shaken an essay by Edgar Allan Poe, [**The Philosophy of Composition**](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/articles/69390/the-philosophy-of-composition), where he claims he engineered his poem **The Raven** backward. “Nevermore,” chosen first, for the mournful sound of the long _o_, and only then a bird invented to carry it.

Turns out the essay is mostly performance. The poem came the way everything comes, in a mess, and the tidy machine Poe described was assembled afterward to look like control. But here is what took me twenty years to understand. The control was fake. The choosing was real. Poe picked “Nevermore” for its sound. The genius was his ear, not the essay. The lie was the tidiness, not the mess.

And that is exactly the thing that did not get cheaper.

AI can make the poem now. It can also write the artist statement. Both ends are cheap. What’s left is whether the choosing was real. Whether you agonized, or performed the agony afterward.

That agony is the conviction. It is the part no model can do on your behalf, because it isn’t output. It’s the having of something to say.

Which is the thing that scares me. Because I don’t actually know if I have it. I’ve spent so long behind the craft that I’m not sure what’s left when you take it away. That’s not a rhetorical worry. I mean it as the plainest fact in this whole piece. I can build anything now, and I am genuinely afraid that I’ll spend the rest of my career finding out I never had anything to say.

You have to become someone with something to say. That’s harder than getting good at the craft ever was, because craft you can practice. To have a point of view, you have to have self-knowledge pointed at a problem, which means knowing what you believe, what you can’t stand, what you’d fight for.

A model can generate a thousand directions. What it cannot do is care which one is right. It will execute your conviction or your lack of it with equal fluency, and never once tell you that you didn’t mean any of it. The caring is yours. It was always going to be the only thing that was.

## Making a private fire

My therapist gave me a worksheet. The kind she hands to everyone, a grid for catching the thought before it runs away with you. I dragged it into Claude Code and an hour later it was an app on my phone, all mine, tuned exactly to the way my own anxiety talks. It worked. It genuinely helped.

And it has bothered me ever since. Finishing is the one act AI has made effortless. It hands you completion for free, which means it hands you the love for free. So I don’t actually know if my app helped. I know I finished it. I know that finishing is the exact moment I’d lose the ability to tell.

The worksheet was a shared thing. Hundreds of people sat with the same grid, the same paper, the small comfort of knowing it was made for more than just them. I took that and made a private fire. If conviction is the thing that didn’t get cheaper, and conviction is solitary, and every one of us can now spin a bespoke version of anything we want, what happens to the things we used to share? A billion private fires, each fitted to the person who lit them, and no warmth that reaches anyone else.

The things that become a zeitgeist were never the most produced. They were the most meant. The song everyone knew that summer spread because one person had something to say and made it for us, and we felt that it was meant. The meaning is the part that still costs something, and it only counts when it’s pointed outward.

The naked designer has one question to answer before reaching for cover again. Not _“Can you build it?”_ You can. We all can now.

_When did you last make something that mattered to someone other than you?_
