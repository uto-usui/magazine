Tue, 17 Nov 2026

My family and I recently spent the morning at the [Toyota Commemorative Museum of Industry and Technology](https://www.tcmit.org/english/).

Interestingly, Toyota didn’t start with cars — its origins were in textile machinery, with Sakichi Toyoda’s inventions in automatic weaving. His son Kiichiro later brought that spirit of innovation into automobile manufacturing, eventually founding Toyota Motor Corporation in 1937.

The museum’s mission statement captures the company’s driving force:

> The Toyota Group wishes to impart to younger generations its belief in the importance of both “Making Things (_Monozukuri_)” and “the Spirit of Being Studious and Creative”. By so doing, it hopes to contribute to the healthy development of the societies and economies of Japan and other countries of the world.

Among the elaborate machinery and car displays, I came across a few unassuming quotes from Kiichiro Toyoda:

1.  > 質実剛健な職工を養成し、着実に仕事ができるようにして、安価で良い車を製造したい念願から出発したのです。  
    > I started with the desire to manufacture low-cost, high-quality automobiles by first and foremost developing skilled craftsmen and enabling them to work steadily.
    
  
3.  > 訓練を積まなかったら、銘刀も鈍刀と同じである。  
    > A machine is like a fine sword — without the training to use it properly, it is the same as a blunt piece of steel.
    
  
5.  > 機械はいつまでたっても機械であり、人間の力によって本当の実力を発揮しうるものである。  
    > Machines are always just machines and can only reach their full potential through the people who use them.
    

— Kiichiro Toyoda

Decades ago, as machines automated more physical labor, Kiichiro contemplated the role of humans. He believed in developing people, not just better machines.

Today, as AI automates more intellectual and creative labor, we face many of the same questions.

* * *

Later that evening, as I read more about Toyota’s history, I discovered one of its guiding philosophies that seemed to capture Kiichiro’s remarks:

> ものづくりは人づくり。  
> Monozukuri wa hitozukuri  
> Making things means developing people.

Where does our skill and judgment come from in the first place? They come, in large part, from doing the work itself.

We don’t only make things because we’re skilled. We become skilled through making them. Through practice. Through exploration. Through thousands of [detours and decisions](https://justinjay.wang/designing-with-code/) along the way. Over time, we develop a sense for what makes something good until that judgment becomes second nature.

But when technology allows us to skip more of the process of making, what happens to the things we make? What happens to us?

Technology can free us to do more meaningful work. It can also fundamentally change how we learn to do that work. It’s not always clear what we’re gaining or giving up.

We tend to evaluate technology by what it enables us to produce. Perhaps we should also consider what it enables us to become.

Because in the process of making things, we make ourselves.
