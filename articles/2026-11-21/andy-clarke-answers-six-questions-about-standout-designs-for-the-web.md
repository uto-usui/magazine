Web designer, author, and [Stuff & Nonsense](https://stuffandnonsense.co.uk/) founder Andy Clarke has released _[Standout Designs for the Web](https://css-tricks.com/product/standout-web-design-for-the-web/)_, a new e-book that encourages designers to move beyond familiar templates and conventional layouts to create more personality-driven websites.

The book draws on Clarke’s thirty years of web design experience. It explores how storytelling, color, typography, imagery, and layout can work together to create websites that people recognize and remember. We asked Andy a few questions about the book and the ideas that went into it.

## Question: What made you want to write this book?

I’ve been designing websites for over thirty years, and I can’t remember a time when designers had more tools, techniques, and technology available to them. Yet, somehow, so many websites look remarkably similar.

I’m not suggesting every website needs a novelty design. Far from it. But I do think that — somewhere along the way—designers have become a little too comfortable with familiar patterns and predictable layouts.

I wrote _Standout Designs for the Web_ to encourage designers to rediscover the creativity and personality that made me fall in love with the web in the first place.

## Question: What’s wrong with websites looking similar? Isn’t familiarity a good thing?

Absolutely. Familiarity helps people understand how websites work, and there’s no reason to make something difficult to use just to be different. The problem is when familiarity makes every design look alike. When you visit a hotel website, an insurance company, or an online shop, you should be able to recognise something of the company behind it.

I call this sameness “commodity design,” and it’s perfectly fine when all you want is fine, but it does little to make a company recognisable or its website memorable. I’m not arguing against using conventions. I’m asking designers to make more thoughtful decisions about when to use them.

## Question: You talk a lot about storytelling. What does that actually mean for web design?

When I talk about storytelling, I don’t mean writing a lengthy company history or filling a website with marketing messages.

Every company has a story worth telling. It might be how it started, the people behind it, its personality, or something unexpected about what it does. Those details can influence everything from the words you write to the colours, typography, illustrations, and layouts you choose.

I spend a lot of time talking to clients because I’m looking for what makes them different. Sometimes, something they mention almost in passing becomes the inspiration for an entire design. The trick is recognising which details are worth developing and turning them into something visual.

## Question: Why did you invent a fake company for getaway drivers to demonstrate your ideas?

Ha! Because I wanted a vehicle for my explanations and to have some fun. Wheelman is a fictional company for getaway drivers, and it gave me the freedom to explore ideas without worrying too much about the practical constraints of a real client project. Its characters, criminal connections, and rather questionable business activities gave me plenty of opportunities to experiment with personality.

But there’s a serious point behind the silliness. I use Wheelman to demonstrate how a company’s character can influence ordinary business pages, e-commerce, editorial content, and even transactional forms. If I can make a donation form for retired criminals interesting, designers have no reason not to bring more personality to everyday websites.

## Question: You challenge the twelve-column grid. Have designers become too dependent on it?

I think twelve-column grids have become the default setting for web design. They’re familiar and useful, but they’re only one way to organise content. When designers begin every project with twelve columns, they’re already limiting their possibilities before they’ve considered what the content needs.

In the book, I explore column, compound, and modular grids; ratios; symmetry versus asymmetry; and other ways to create rhythm and movement across a page. I’m not suggesting designers abandon column grids, but I want them to understand layout well enough to choose the right type and combine several—to make their layouts more interesting.

## Question: What’s the one thing you’d like someone to do differently after reading your book?

I’d like them to approach their next project with a little more curiosity. Before opening Figma or Sketch, choosing a typeface, or deciding on a layout, I’d like them to spend more time discovering what makes the company they’re designing for interesting.

Then I’d encourage them to question their first design decisions. Does this layout suit the content, or is it just familiar? Does this typography express the company’s personality? Can an illustration communicate something more effectively than an AI-generated image or a stock photo?

I don’t expect every website to be extraordinary, and I certainly don’t think every design needs to break conventions. But I’d like designers to feel more confident making decisions that give their work character.
